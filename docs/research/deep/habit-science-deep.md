## 1. What a habit is

Gardner 2015 (Health Psychology Review; scoping review, 136 studies, 8 reviews) fixed the definition now standard in health psychology: a *process* by which a stimulus generates an impulse to act through a learned stimulus–response association. Not the behaviour, not frequency, not identity. The SRHI mixed in frequency and identity items; the SRBAI (CF §6) isolates automaticity. Hagger et al. 2015 (Annals of Behavioral Medicine): any self-report index captures the *experience* of habit and can be inflated by frequency; a proxy, not a measure of the S–R link.

Wood, Quinn & Kashy 2002 (JPSP; two hourly-beeper diary studies, students): about 43% of daily actions were performed almost daily in the same context, typically while thinking about something else. **Corr**; the source of the "43%" in Wood's *Good Habits, Bad Habits*.

Wood & Rünger 2016 (Annual Review of Psychology) give three habit–goal interfaces: habits form *while* pursuing goals through repetition in stable context; once formed, habit is the efficient default; and people infer intention from habit frequency. Wood, Mazar & Neal 2022 call the systems "separate but interacting"; De Houwer et al. 2023 reply that goal mediation is not excluded. Both accept the practical facts: stable context plus repetition builds automaticity, and intentions alone do not undo it.

Mazar & Wood 2022 (Psychological Science; online experiment N = 809; EMA of coffee drinking N = 112): habit strength matched or beat mood and intention in predicting behaviour, yet participants explained behaviour by inner states, even when paid for accuracy. With DP §7: a user's story of *why* they skipped is weaker evidence than their barrier-tap pattern.

Gardner, de Bruijn & Lally 2011 (**MA**, 21 datasets, nutrition and physical activity): habit–behaviour r = .44 fixed, .46 random; in most of nine tests intention mattered less as habit strengthened. Gardner, Phillips & Judah 2016 and Phillips & Gardner 2016 (Health Psychology): the frequency effect lives in *instigation* (automatically deciding to start), not execution; instigation habit predicted exercise frequency, execution did not. So word the automaticity item around starting: "deciding to go for the walk is something I do automatically".

## 2. Dual-process control: what Dickinson licenses

Dickinson 1985 (Phil Trans R Soc B) and Adams & Dickinson 1981 defined the modes in rats: goal-directed action is sensitive to outcome devaluation and contingency degradation; habit is not; overtraining shifts control from the first to the second. Yin & Knowlton 2006 mapped them to dorsomedial versus dorsolateral striatum. **Lab, animal, replicated.**

In humans the evidence is thin. Tricomi, Balleine & O'Doherty 2009 was the one demonstration that three days of overtraining produced devaluation insensitivity. de Wit et al. 2018 (JEP: General) report five failures across four paradigms including two direct replications; "habit" deficits in compulsive patients may instead be impaired goal-directed control. Pool et al. 2022 (Learning & Memory, multilaboratory): no main effect of training; low-stress participants became habitual with training, high-stress ones already were. De Houwer et al. 2018 (Motivation Science): the tests are valid only if sensitive and aimed at the outcome that actually controls behaviour, so human habit evidence "might be overestimated". Buabang, Donegan, Rafei & Gillan 2025 (Trends in Cognitive Sciences): making habits needs repetition, reinforcement, *disengagement of goal-directed processing* and stable context; breaking needs weakened S–R links, cue avoidance, goal-directed inhibition or a competing S–R link; the balance differs by person (stress, anxiety).

**Verdict.** Dual-process is solid as description and in animals; the claim that days of repetition make human behaviour goal-insensitive has not replicated. Reliable reading: *instigation* becomes automatic while the goal system stays intact. Adler never fights a habit that "ignores goals"; it builds a cue–response link and lowers the load on deliberate control.

## 3. Reward and dopamine, as far as the evidence goes

Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997 (Science): dopamine neurons encode reward-prediction error; **Lab, primate, replicated**, and the basis for "reward-based learning" in Wood & Rünger. Human evidence that dopamine drives everyday habit formation is indirect (imaging in the non-replicated paradigms); Buabang 2025 do not rest on it. CF §5 already bans neuroscience in copy.

What replicates behaviourally is that reward *experienced at the action* speeds habit growth:

- Judah et al. 2018 (BMC Psychology; N = 118, 16 weeks, flossing and vitamin C, SEM): intrinsic motivation and pleasure moderated the repetition → habit slope; perceived utility and benefits did nothing; strong intentions *weakened* the slope; context stability mediated. **Field, single group.**
- Ma et al. 2023 (**MA**, IJBNPA, 10 studies): habit-formation interventions raised physical-activity habit strength SMD 0.31 (CI 0.14–0.48); meta-regression: problem solving β = 0.36 (CI 0.17–0.55), *social reward* β = −0.40 (CI −0.74 to 0.06); effects larger at ≤ 12 weeks.
- Stawarz, Cox & Blandford 2015 (CHI; 4-week study): added positive reinforcement was ineffective for automaticity.
- Shen, Fishbach & Hsee 2015 (JCR, 4 studies): people invest more for a 50/50 chance of $2 or $1 than a sure $2, only when process-focused. No independent replication found; it is the slot-machine mechanism the APA advisory warns against (CF §5).

**Verdict.** The reward that matters is intrinsic and noticed immediately: pleasure, relief, satisfaction of enactment. Points, badges, applause and variable rewards are neutral to slightly negative. Fogg's "celebrate" (CF rank 9) survives only as a one-line prompt to notice what was good, straight after.

## 4. Cues: type, position, time of day, frequency, duration

**Type.** Keller et al. 2021 (BJHP; **RCT**, N = 192 randomised, 135 analysed, 84 daily reports, self-chosen nutrition behaviour): routine-based ("after breakfast") and time-based ("at 8 am") plans both raised automaticity and enactment with *no between-condition difference*; habit-formers took a median of 59 days to peak; repeated plan enactment, within and between persons, was the key predictor. Earlier cross-sectional work had favoured routine cues. Stawarz 2015: phone reminders produced repetition but *hindered* automaticity; event-based cues raised it; of 115 habit apps, none supported event cues. Renfree et al. 2016 (CHI EA): reminders and streaks create dependency; abandon the app, abandon the behaviour. Baretta et al. 2024/25 (HabitWalk; micro-randomised, N = 24, 112 days): trajectories varied widely; cued walks strengthened habit; commitment plus reminder looked best, tentatively.

**Position.** Judah, Gardner & Aunger 2013 (BJHP; flossing, 8-month follow-up): flossing *after* brushing produced stronger habits than before; prospective-memory ability predicted frequency; positive attitude predicted habit independent of repetition. **Field, small.** Put the new action at the end of an existing sequence.

**Time of day.** Fournier et al. 2017 (Health Psychology; N = 48, 90 days, randomised morning vs evening stretch): automaticity at 106 vs 154 days, mediated by cortisol. Singh 2024 (AH) agrees across studies. One small trial, consistent direction.

**Frequency.** Kaushal & Rhodes 2015 (J Behav Med; N = 111 new gym members, 12 weeks): at least four bouts a week for six weeks was the minimum for habit to establish. **Corr.**

**Duration by behaviour.** Buyalskaya et al. 2023 (PNAS; 12 M gym visits, 40 M hospital hand-washes; per-person predictability curves): gym attendance became habitual at a median of 122–226 days (4–7 months); hand-washing after about 225–250 episodes, i.e. weeks. With Keller (59), Lally (66) and Singh (59–66, up to 335): two months for simple daily acts, four to seven for effortful ones with rarer cues. van der Weiden et al. 2020 (Frontiers; three-month field study): habit rose in consistent performers; trait self-control did not change the rate.

## 5. Habit discontinuity: transitions as windows

Verplanken's hypothesis: a context change breaks the cue, behaviour is briefly deliberated, and values can steer it.

- Verplanken, Walker, Davies & Jurasek 2008 (university employees): recently moved *and* environmentally concerned staff drove less. **Corr.**
- Walker, Thomas & Verplanken 2015 (office relocation; 19 months before, 1 and 4 weeks after): old-mode automaticity decayed over weeks while the new mode grew; both coexisted. **Field.**
- Verplanken & Roy 2016 (**Field experiment**, N = 800, moved × intervention, 25 behaviours, 8 weeks): the intervention improved behaviour only in households that had moved within 1–13 weeks; the window closed at about three months.
- Thomas, Poortinga & Sautkina 2016 (PLoS ONE; Understanding Society, N = 18,053): among the environmentally concerned, predicted car commuting fell to about .54 in the first month after moving and rose over the following year. **Panel.**

**Verdict.** Consistent across one field experiment, one large panel and quasi-experiments. The window is roughly 90 days, works through *value activation* (only people who cared changed), and cuts both ways: existing good habits lose their cues too.

## 6. Stress and low willpower

Neal, Wood & Drolet 2013 (JPSP; five studies, N = 65, 72, 59, 134, 164): in exam weeks or after depletion, strong habits were performed *more* (strong-habit health-bar eating 1.05 vs 0.55 per week, F(1, 80) = 8.38), weak habits were not, and the boost ignored whether the habit served the person's goals. Galla & Duckworth 2015 (JPSP; six studies, N = 2,274): trait self-control's benefits were mediated by beneficial habits; high-self-control people reported *less* effortful inhibition. Pool 2022 (§2) shows the same shift in the lab. CF rank 8 is the same lesson from the other side.

## 7. Tracking and digital interventions

Monitoring raises goal attainment (Harkin, CF §1) and works better with goal setting, feedback and review (Michie et al. 2009; **MA**, 122 evaluations). Neither concerns automaticity. Stawarz and Renfree (§4) show tracking-plus-reminder apps make the app the cue; Gardner & Rebar 2019 (15 habit interventions) found short-term effects only; Gardner et al. 2021/23 (Psychology & Health, 21 questions): "the fundamental assumption that making or breaking habits will lead to lasting behaviour change remains largely untested", because trials use no-treatment controls and rarely test mediation. JMIR 2024 (41 digital habit studies): commonest techniques were self-monitoring, goal setting and prompts; 88% ran under six months. Pinder et al. 2018 (ACM Computing Surveys) reach the same diagnosis.

**Verdict.** Tracking is the coach's instrument and the user's feedback, not the engine. The record must be retrospective (an evening "did it happen" is a record; a morning ping is a cue), and it should *fade* as automaticity rises so the app never becomes the cue.

## 8. Breaking habits

- **Vigilant monitoring.** Quinn, Pascoe, Wood & Neal 2010 (PSPB): two event-sampling diaries (N = 99, 7 days; N = 33, 14 days): for *strong habits* the most effective strategy was vigilant monitoring ("don't do it", watching for slips), beating stimulus control (M = 2.81 on a 7-point success scale); for *temptations*, stimulus control and distraction beat monitoring. An experiment (N = 65, trained word-pair habits, process dissociation) showed monitoring raised cognitive control without weakening the habit. Overall success means of 3.6–3.9/7: control of habits is modest.
- **Planning against habit.** Webb, Sheeran & Luszczynska 2009 (BJSP; lab plus smoking field study): if-then plans helped less when the habit was strong. Adriaanse et al. 2011 (**MA**, 23 studies): d = 0.51 for promoting healthy eating versus 0.29 for reducing unhealthy eating; Adriaanse, Gollwitzer et al. 2011 (PSPB): plans work when the if-part names the person's *actual* trigger.
- **Friction.** Hollands et al. 2019 (**Cochrane**, 24 studies, food only): lower proximity cut consumption SMD −0.60 (12 RCTs, N = 1,098, ~19% per occasion, low certainty); lower availability cut selection SMD −1.13 (3 RCTs, N = 154, low). Wood 2024 (Current Directions) names three levers that change habits without changing intentions: reward for the new habit, disruption of the old cue, friction. Wood's gym-distance figures in the book are unsourced; Cochrane is the citable version.

**Verdict.** For an unwanted habit: find the real cue (one week of "when did it happen?" taps), change the environment first, then an if-then plan naming that cue and a replacement, and vigilant monitoring only where the cue cannot be avoided. Expect partial success and say so.

## 10. Implications for Adler

**Questions to ask.**
- Intake and quarterly: "Anything changing in the next three months: moving, new job, new term, baby, new relationship, long travel?" A yes opens a *transition window*: best time to start one action, and a warning to re-anchor existing ones.
- Action design (CF Phase 3): "What already happens every day that this could come *right after*?" End of a sequence; clock time only if there is no routine.
- After the first completions: "What was good about it, just now?" One line, no points.
- Bad habit: "When did it happen, where, right after what?" for seven days before proposing anything.
- Weekly automaticity, worded for instigation: "Starting X is something I do without thinking. 1–7."

**Rules and thresholds.**
- **Reminders are not cues.** D3 stands. If a user asks for one, frame it as scaffolding to remove; drop it once SRBAI ≥ 5/7 and say so at set-up.
- **Fade tracking.** SRBAI ≥ 5/7 for two consecutive weeks → daily log optional, weekly review only. Matches HE "track the task, not yourself".
- **Time to automaticity is per behaviour.** Simple daily act (< 5 min, daily cue): "about two months". Effortful act with rarer cue (gym, deep-work block): "four to seven months". Never one number in copy.
- **Frequency floor.** Habit goals: at least 4 days a week. Fewer is fine for a project goal's "work on it" action, but do not promise automaticity.
- **Stress rule.** Two consecutive weeks of "no energy" or a reported crisis: hold, do not add or grow; protect the cue of established actions; wellbeing check (CF §2). Depletion boosts strong habits and starves new deliberate ones.
- **Transition rule.** Within 13 weeks of a move, job change or new term: offer to start one action and re-anchor every existing action to a cue that exists in the new context.
- **Breaking-habit sequence.** Cue diary → environment change → if-then with the real cue and a replacement → vigilant monitoring only where the cue is unavoidable. Never lead with willpower.
- **Reward hygiene.** No social reward, badges, variable rewards or "you earned it". Affirm effort and strategy (CF).
- **Morning bias, lightly.** With no preference, propose a morning anchor; never move an evening habit that works.

**Adapting to one person over time.**
- Store per action: cue type, anchor and position, time of day, weekly enactment, SRBAI slope, reward line. The hypothesis log (D3) then tests cue changes as Keller did: enactment seven days before versus after.
- Learn the person's *formation rate*: days to SRBAI ≥ 4 on the first action is the prior for the next (HabitWalk: wide variance; Judah 2018: pleasure moves the slope). "Your first one took ten weeks" beats promising 66 days.
- Learn their *stress signature* (which weeks produced "no energy", what happened to established habits) and *cue reliability* ("forgot" clusters mean the anchor is not daily; switch anchor, CF re-cue rule).
- Believe taps over narrative (Mazar & Wood): when the weekly story and the barrier pattern disagree, reflect the pattern.

**Avoid.** Neuroscience in copy; a single "days to habit" number; reminders as default cue; tracking that never fades; streaks; social or variable rewards; framing a strong bad habit as weak will; starting new actions in a high-stress fortnight; leaving actions un-anchored through a move.

## 11. Conflicts and open questions

1. **Goal-independence of habits.** Wood, Mazar & Neal 2022 yes; De Houwer 2023 unproven; de Wit 2018 and Pool 2022 show lab induction is fragile. Evidence favours *descriptive* dual-process (automatic instigation, intact goals). Do not tell users "your habit no longer depends on your goals".
2. **Routine versus time cues.** Cross-sectional work favoured routines; the only RCT found no difference. Favour the RCT; keep routines as default because they survive schedule changes, not because they form faster.
3. **Reminders.** Stawarz 2015 (hinder) versus HabitWalk (commitment plus reminder best, N = 24). Stawarz is larger and matches Renfree; keep reminders off by default, allow opt-in scaffolding that is removed later.
4. **Reward.** Ma 2023's social-reward CI crosses zero; Judah 2018 is single-group; Shen 2015 unreplicated. Safe reading: intrinsic, immediate, noticed.
5. **Does habit formation produce lasting change?** Largely untested (Gardner 2021/23); 88% of digital trials under six months. Adler's cohort at weeks 12 and 26 (CF §6) would be rare evidence; design the retention analysis now.
6. **Two-miss rule.** Still no study. Walker 2015 shows automaticity decays over weeks after a context break, which argues against alarm at two misses.
7. **Stress as moderator of induction (Pool) versus booster of existing habits (Neal).** Not a contradiction: stress shifts control toward whatever is already habitual.
8. **Open, from Gardner et al. 2021/23:** which cues support real-world habits; optimal routine position; what strength sustains behaviour; which rewards quicken formation; how far a link must degrade to break; whether degraded habits reform faster; whether explaining habit to users helps. Adler's logged hypotheses can contribute if the schema keeps cue, position and reward fields.
