# Maintenance over months and years: what survives, what predicts it, and what a coach can do about it

## 1. What maintenance is, and why initiation theories fail at it

**Kwasnicka, Dombrowski, White & Sniehotta 2016** (Health Psychology Review; 117 theories screened, 100 included, 25 theory experts consulted; **D, systematic**). Definition, verbatim: "The continuous performance of a behaviour following an initial intentional change at a level that significantly differs from the baseline performance in the intended direction" — note that it contains no time cut-off, deliberately. Five themes, each a hypothesis about why people keep going:

1. **Maintenance motives**: at least one *sustained* motive, meaning enjoyment of the behaviour, satisfaction with outcomes, intrinsic motivation, or identity congruence. Initiation motives (outcome expectations, fear, a trigger event) are consumed by success.
2. **Self-regulation**: continued monitoring, coping with barriers and lapses; effortful, so it fails when resources fall.
3. **Habit**: automatic response to stable cues; the only theme that gets cheaper with time.
4. **Resources**: psychological and physical (energy, mood, money, time); maintenance fails when they are depleted.
5. **Environment and social influence**: cues, availability, other people.

The authors' verdict, verbatim: explanations of maintenance "focus on the differential nature and role of motives, self-regulation, resources (psychological and physical), habits, and environmental and social influences from initiation to maintenance", and there are "distinct patterns of theoretical explanations for behaviour change and for behaviour change maintenance". *Correction (2026-09-02):* a full-text search of the paper finds no support for the widely repeated gloss that maintenance "relies increasingly on habit development and environmental facilitation rather than active motivation"; that sentence is a secondary-source paraphrase and must not be quoted as Kwasnicka. The paper's own limitations: health-behaviour bias, and most theories were never tested on maintenance.

**Rothman's phase models (D).** Rothman 2000 (Health Psychology): initiation depends on "favorable expectations regarding future outcomes"; maintenance on "perceived satisfaction with received outcomes." Rothman, Baldwin & Hertel 2004 split the course into initial response, continued response, maintenance, and habit. Rothman, Sheeran & Wood 2009 (Annals of Behavioral Medicine) cross reflective vs automatic processes with initiation vs maintenance and argue each cell needs its own technique. **Rhodes & Sui 2021** (Frontiers in Psychology, narrative review) push further: maintenance is not a stage but "a process marked by a shift in the mechanisms of action determining behavioral performance, that engender greater perceived behavioral enactment efficiency"; the initiation constructs never stop mattering, they just cost less. **Murray et al. 2018** (Annals of Behavioral Medicine; 39 PA trials with ≥ 6-month follow-up): only 12 ran formal mediation tests; 34% of 413 mediator-change tests were significant; "behavioral processes of change" mediated in 5 of 6 tests; no agreed definition of maintenance exists. Adler needs an operational one (§8).

## 2. Satisfaction with outcomes: the Rothman line of evidence

- **Baldwin et al. 2006** (Health Psychology; N = 591 smokers, 8-week programme, 15-month follow-up; **C, prospective**): self-efficacy predicted quit status for *initiators*; satisfaction with quitting predicted quit status for *maintainers* at 2, 9 and 15 months.
- **Hertel et al. 2008** (Health Psychology; **B, RCT**): smokers randomised to optimistic vs modest expectation messaging. Favourable expectations raised *initiation*; satisfaction, not expectations, predicted *maintained* abstinence over 15 months; the manipulation's effect on beliefs was moderated by prior cessation success.
- **Finch et al. 2005** (Health Psychology; N = 349, **B, RCT**): "optimistic" vs "balanced" weight-loss messaging shifted cognitions but produced no difference in weight at 18 months; independent of arm, both expectations and satisfaction correlated with loss. Balanced framing cost nothing.
- **Baldwin, Rothman & Jeffery 2009** (Annals of Behavioral Medicine; 18-month random-coefficient models): nine of ten weight-related experiences covaried with satisfaction within-person, explaining 21–38% of within-person variance, more than between-person differences. The smoking companion paper (Psychology & Health 2009; seven waves): eight of nine experiences related, 39–44% of within-person variance; current smoking mattered early, *feedback from others* mattered later.
- **Voils et al. 2017** (Annals of Internal Medicine; N = 222 randomised after losing ≥ 4 kg in a 16-week group programme; **B**): a mostly telephone maintenance programme built on satisfaction with outcomes, relapse-prevention planning, self-monitoring and social support. Estimated mean regain 0.75 kg vs 2.36 kg at 56 weeks (difference 1.60 kg, 95% CI 0.07 to 3.13; p = 0.040); no secondary outcome (calories, walking, moderate activity) differed. The only trial built directly on Rothman's thesis — and the sample is Veterans Affairs patients, "mostly middle-aged white men", so treat it as a direction, not a promise-able magnitude.
- **Kwasnicka et al. 2017** (Psychology & Health; N-of-1, 8 adults, 6 months daily EMA; **C**): daily adherence to the maintenance plan tracked maintenance motivation, satisfaction, self-regulation, habit and stable environment; *resources were inconsistent across people*.
- **ten Broeke & Adriaanse 2026**, *Sticking to New Year's Resolutions* (PsyArXiv preprint, 6 January 2026, not peer-reviewed; N = 183 approximately representative Dutch adults, four waves over five months; **C**): 65% of resolutions were approach-oriented, mostly activity or nutrition; adherence held near 60% for approach goals and declined for avoidance goals; the strongest and most consistent predictors in mixed-effects models were habit strength, satisfaction with the change, self-efficacy and self-monitoring. Self-chosen goals in a general-population sample: the closest published design to Adler's use case, and the closest sample to Adler's audience.
- **Huffman et al. 2021** (Health Psychology Review; 16 studies, adults ≥ 55): self-determination and PA maintenance r = .19 (.13–.25); satisfaction, enjoyment and identity each had fewer than five studies. The motives theme is under-measured, not disproven.

**Verdict.** One RCT, several prospective cohorts, consistent direction, modest size. Satisfaction is about *experienced* outcomes against expectations, so it is the empirical form of the false-hope problem (LR §3): inflated expectations buy initiation and sell maintenance.

**Reading.** When contact stops, roughly half to two-thirds of the end-of-programme effect remains at a year and it keeps shrinking; effects that persist belong to programmes that kept in touch. Three maintenance-phase RCTs sharpen this: **Svetkey et al. 2008** (JAMA; N = 1,032, 30 months): monthly brief personal contact regained 4.0 kg, an interactive website 5.2 kg, self-directed 5.5 kg; the technology arm gave "early but transient benefit." **Perri et al. 2008** (Arch Intern Med; N = 234): telephone and face-to-face extended care regained 1.2 kg each vs 3.7 kg for education. **Sniehotta et al. 2019** (PLOS Medicine; NULevel, N = 288): connected scales plus automated tailored SMS feedback regained 1.8 kg, exactly matching the newsletter control. Automated feedback without a conversation is not extended care.

**The largest digital test, and it is null (A).** *NoHoW* (Stubbs, Duarte, Dakin, Palmeira, Sniehotta et al. 2026, JMIR; 2 × 2 factorial RCT, N = 1,627 adults in the UK, Denmark and Portugal who had lost ≥ 5% of body weight in the previous 12 months) randomised a digital toolkit targeting self-regulation/motivation (n = 403), emotion regulation (n = 416), both (n = 408), or an **active control** (generic content, a Fitbit, regular self-weighing; n = 400). At 12 months (76% retained) no arm differed from the active control on weight; per-protocol regain was 0.14 kg in men and 0.54 kg in women; nearly half the ITT sample regained; no cardiometabolic outcome moved; a small subgroup effect for motivation in men is not clinically meaningful. This is the biggest digital weight-loss-maintenance trial in existence and its comparator already contained the monitoring loop — the honest reading is that *self-weighing plus a tracker is most of what digital maintenance products deliver*, and the content on top of it did not add measurable weight benefit.

**The strongest negative in the maintenance literature (A).** Livingstone-Banks et al. 2019 (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews; 81 studies, 69,094 participants; 22 at high risk of bias) asked whether relapse-prevention interventions keep quitters quit. In the cleanest tests — trials that randomised people who were *already abstinent* — 12 analyses were run: the behavioural ones (skills for coping with urges, extra support contacts) showed no benefit; only extended pharmacotherapy did (varenicline in assisted abstainers RR 1.23, 95% CI 1.08–1.41, I² = 82%, moderate certainty; NRT in unaided abstainers RR 1.24, 1.04–1.47). Maintenance-specific *content* has never been shown to work in the best-measured domain there is. What survives across domains is continued contact, dose, and (in smoking) drugs.

**A non-weight comparison (A).** Vittengl, Clark, Dunn & Jarrett 2007 (Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology; 28 studies, N = 1,880): after responding to acute-phase cognitive therapy for depression, 29% relapse within one year and 54% within two — comparable to other depression-specific psychotherapies and *lower* than after pharmacotherapy. Continuation-phase CT reduced relapse by 21% at the end of continuation and 29% at follow-up versus assessment only, and by 12% and 14% versus other active continuation treatments. Same shape as weight: a lower-dose continuation of the same work buys a real but partial reduction in relapse, and stopping entirely costs the most.

**The dissent on contact (A).** Hartmann-Boyce, Johns, Jebb & Aveyard 2014 (Obesity Reviews; 37 RCTs, > 16,000 participants) meta-regressed *weight-loss* programme features on 12-month weight: neither more frequent contact (−0.3 kg, 95% CI −0.7 to 0.2, p = 0.25) nor in-person delivery (0.0 kg, −1.8 to 1.8) predicted outcome, while calorie counting (−3.3 kg) and dietitian contact (−1.5 kg) did. I² showed 93% of variance was between-programme. Read alongside Middleton, Perri and Svetkey, the resolution is phase-specific: extra contact *during* an initiation programme adds little; contact that continues *after* it is where the maintenance effect lives (Conflicts §6).

## 4. Weight-loss maintenance: the best-studied case

**Base rates (C).** Anderson et al. 2001 (AJCN; 29 US studies): five years after a structured programme the average person keeps > 3 kg, about 3% of body weight; 17% of the initial loss after balanced diets, 29% after very-low-energy diets. Fildes et al. 2015 (AJPH; UK primary care, N = 176,495 obese adults): of those who lost 5%, 52.7% had regained it within two years and 78.0% within five; the annual chance of reaching normal weight was 1 in 210 for men and 1 in 124 for women. Hall & Kahan 2018 call early loss, plateau and progressive regain "near-ubiquitous" and physiological. Wing & Phelan 2005: about 20% of overweight people who lose ≥ 10% keep it a year.

**Long RCTs (A/B).** Look AHEAD 2014 (Obesity; N = 5,145 with type 2 diabetes, intervention *continued* for eight years): 4.7% vs 2.1% loss at year 8; 50.3% vs 35.7% kept ≥ 5%; 26.9% vs 17.2% kept ≥ 10%; keepers practised the weight-control behaviours more. DPP Outcomes Study 2009 (Lancet; N = 2,766 followed 10 years): lifestyle weight loss "partly regained," yet diabetes incidence stayed 34% lower. Outcomes can outlast the behaviour that produced them, and long-run "success" in trials came with contact that never stopped.

**Social and home environment (B).** Wing & Jeffery 1999 (JCCP; N = 166): recruited with friends plus social-support training, 95% completed and 66% kept the full loss from month 4 to 10, against 76% and 24% alone. Gorin et al. 2008 (IJO; 357 spouse pairs): untreated spouses of intensive-intervention participants lost 2.2 kg vs 0.2 kg, tracking fewer high-fat foods at home. Gorin et al. 2013 (Health Psychology; N = 201): adding a home-environment package (equipment, portion plates, a household partner) beat standard treatment at 6 months, equal at 18 months overall, still better for women.

**The National Weight Control Registry (C).** Klem et al. 1997 (N = 784): mean loss 30 kg, kept ≥ 13.6 kg for 5+ years; about half used a programme, half did it alone; 77% used diet *and* exercise; over half named a triggering event; 42% found maintaining *easier* than losing. Wing & Phelan 2005: about an hour a day of activity, low-fat lower-calorie diet, breakfast, regular self-weighing, and the same eating pattern on weekdays and weekends. Thomas et al. 2014 (AJPM; N = 2,886 followed 10 years): 31.3 kg lost at entry, 23.8 kg still off at 5 years, 23.1 kg at 10; > 87% still ≥ 10% down at both points; regain tracked *declines* in leisure activity, dietary restraint and self-weighing and rising fat intake. Klem et al. 2000 (Obesity Research; N = 931, mean 6.8 years): longer maintainers used fewer strategies, reported less effort and attention; *pleasure* from exercise or eating did not vary with duration. McGuire et al. 1999 (JCCP): 35% regained within a year; risks were a loss under two years old, a larger loss, depression, disinhibition, binge eating; regainers cut energy expenditure and restraint. Phelan et al. 2003 (AJCN; N = 2,400, 2 years): 65.7% gained in year one; of those, 11.0% got back to baseline by year two; of the 25.5% who regained ≥ 5%, 4.7% did; "recovery from even minor weight regain was uncommon." Ogden et al. 2012 (Obesity; N = 2,228): four clusters: 50.5% weight-stable, exercise-conscious, satisfied; 26.9% struggling since childhood, using the *most* strategies, with more stress and depression; 12.7% succeeded first time with least difficulty; 9.9% older, less exercise-reliant. Catenacci et al. 2008: mean 2,621 kcal/week of activity but 25% report under 1,000. Paixão et al. 2020 (Obesity Reviews; five national registries, 52 papers): physical activity was the most consistent correlate; healthy food at home, breakfast, vegetables, limiting sugar and fat recur across countries.

**Limits.** Registries are self-selected survivors with self-reported behaviour and no counterfactual: they show what maintainers *do*, not what would make a non-maintainer one. Varkevisser et al. 2019 (Obesity Reviews; 67 studies, 124 determinants): demographics do not predict maintenance; behaviours and cognitions that lower intake, raise expenditure and *monitor the balance* do. Teixeira et al. 2015 (BMC Medicine; 35 studies): autonomous motivation, self-efficacy and self-regulation skills are the consistent mediators of weight and PA outcomes; flexible restraint and body image help in the medium term; no consistent diet mediator.

## 5. Identity and self-concept

- **Verplanken & Sui 2019** (Frontiers in Psychology; Study 1 N = 90, Study 2 N = 482; **C plus one manipulation**): within-person habit–identity correlation median .46 (Study 1) and .65 (Study 2 control); asking people which *values* a behaviour served raised it to .71 (d = 0.21). Habit–identity integration correlated with self-esteem (r = .45) and promotion focus (r = .68). "The causal flow ... is unknown and may well be bi-directional."
- **Zhu et al. 2025** (Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being; 32 effects, N = 13,340): habit–identity r = .55; higher with explicit identity measures; "limited predictive studies imply the relationship might not be unidirectional." (Cited in AH; extended here.)
- **Rhodes, Kaushal & Quinlan 2016** (Health Psychology Review; 62 datasets): PA identity/schema–behaviour r = .44 (.39–.48), robust to study features; identity moderates intention→behaviour. **Caldwell et al. 2024** (IJBNPA; International Weight Control Registry, N = 1,709): four-item exerciser and healthy-eater identity scales; exercise identity–self-reported leisure activity r(735) = .52, healthy-eater identity–cognitive restraint r(744) = .42. Self-defined successful maintainers scored higher than regainers on both, but the between-group effects were small to medium — exercise identity η² = 0.05 for maintaining ≥ 5% loss versus not, healthy-eater identity η² = 0.10 across the three weight-loss categories. Identity separates maintainers from regainers by considerably less than the correlations imply.
- **Rhodes et al. 2025** (Health Psychology Review; 40 effects, N = 4,939; **A**): interventions move PA identity g = 0.18 (0.11–0.24) while moving behaviour g = 0.61 in the same trials. Identity moves less and later than behaviour. **van Vliet et al. 2026** (Systematic Reviews; 20 studies): about half of identity-focused interventions showed positive effects on identity or behaviour, the rest null, none negative; "optimal design and operationalization remain uncertain."
- **Gillman, Stevens & Bryan 2021** (Psychology of Sport and Exercise; N = 276 inactive women, 16 weeks supervised; **B**): exercise identity rose (t = 7.94), equally across four dose conditions; each unit of identity change predicted +16 min/week of exercise at the 6-month follow-up (b = 16.76, p = .023). **Rhodes et al. 2025** (APHWB; parents of young children, N = 184, 3 months): identity rose within six weeks in team sport, habit rose later and less.
- **Porter et al. 2024** (Psychology of Sport and Exercise; N = 1,451 adolescents, four annual waves, cross-lagged; **C**): identity predicted later activity at every wave; activity did not predict later identity. **Houser-Marko & Sheldon 2006** (PSPB; three studies): endorsing "self-as-doer" statements ("I am an exerciser") predicted persistence beyond expectancy, self-concordance, commitment and neuroticism; a priming study reproduced it.

**Verdict.** Identity is a strong, replicated *correlate* of maintenance (r ≈ .44–.55), rises as a consequence of sustained behaviour (Gillman; Rhodes 2025), and in one panel precedes it (Porter). No trial shows that *instilling* identity adds maintenance beyond the behaviour change that produced it, and the public-declaration hazard stands (CF §1). The safe direction is the AH audit's: identity is evidence accumulated from actions, reflected back, never assigned.

## 6. Habit, motives, resources, environment: what each contributes

**Habit versus deliberate maintenance.** The registry data settle the "effortless habit" question: a decade in, maintainers still self-weigh, still restrain, and regain the moment those *deliberate* behaviours lapse (Thomas 2014), while effort and attention fall with years (Klem 2000). With HS §1 (instigation automatic, execution a skill) the picture is: starting becomes automatic, monitoring stays light but deliberate, and the cost of both declines. ten Broeke & Adriaanse 2026 found habit strength *and* self-monitoring predicting resolution adherence at the same time. Rothman, Sheeran & Wood's matrix says the same in theory.

**Motives.** Teixeira et al. 2012 (IJBNPA; 66 studies): identified regulation predicts adoption more strongly, *intrinsic* motivation predicts long-term adherence more strongly. Silva et al. 2011 (MSSE; N = 221 women, 1-year SDT intervention plus 2-year follow-up; **B**): autonomous exercise motivation at 1–2 years predicted weight at 3 years, partly through moderate-to-vigorous activity (effect ratio .42). Rhodes & Kates 2015 (Annals of Behavioral Medicine; 24 studies): affect *during* moderate exercise predicts future activity; post-exercise affect does not. Hunter et al. 2018 (IJBNPA; cluster RCT, N = 853): a points-for-steps loyalty scheme raised identified and intrinsic motivation at four weeks and produced *fewer* steps at six months (−336/day); only 39% of points were redeemed. Klem 2000's null for pleasure is about *duration* of maintenance, not whether pleasure predicts persistence; the two are compatible.

**Resources.** Kwasnicka's fourth theme is the least consistent within person (N-of-1) and the most consistent between groups: McGuire's regainers were more depressed and disinhibited; Ogden's strugglers carried more stress. Norcross & Vangarelli 1988 (N = 200 resolvers, two years): successful resolvers averaged 14 slips, triggered by "lack of personal control, excessive stress, and negative emotions." Resources decide when self-regulation fails, so the HS stress rule is a maintenance rule.

**Environment and social.** Wing & Jeffery, Gorin 2008/2013 and Paixão's "healthy foods available at home" say the home is a maintenance variable; Wood 2005 and Verplanken & Roy (HS §5) say a context change breaks maintained behaviour; Norcross & Vangarelli: social support and interpersonal strategies did not predict success before six months and did after; Baldwin 2009: feedback from others drives satisfaction *later* in the course. Social support is a maintenance-phase ingredient more than an initiation one.

## Implications for Adler

**Define the phase.** Adler's operational maintenance gate, consistent with AH: SRBAI ≥ 5/7 and ≥ 80% of intended days for 8 weeks. At the gate the coach *changes what it asks*, not whether it asks. Initiation questions (expectations, confidence, plan) give way to maintenance questions (satisfaction, effort, enjoyment, identity, environment). The coaching dose drops (AH), the loop does not end; Svetkey, Perri, Middleton and Krebs show the effect belongs to whoever keeps in touch, and NULevel shows an automated summary is not keeping in touch. D5's weekly review with a proposed change is the personal-contact analogue and must stay conversational.

**Questions to add.**
- Intake, alongside importance and confidence: "What do you expect to be different in two months, honestly?" Then state the typical course in one sentence (Finch: balanced framing costs nothing; Hertel: inflated expectations buy initiation and sell maintenance).
- Monthly from week 8: "Is this giving you what you wanted? 0–10." and "What's better since you started, that you didn't expect?" (Rothman; Baldwin 2009: satisfaction tracks noticed experiences, and *other people's feedback* later on.)
- Monthly from week 8: "How much effort does it take now? 0–10." Expect a fall (Klem). Flat or rising effort by month four with steady adherence means a cue, environment or resource problem, not a motivation problem.
- Quarterly: "Which part, if any, do you enjoy while doing it?" (Rhodes & Kates: in-task affect predicts; post-task does not). None by month three → change the *form* of the behaviour, keep the goal (Teixeira: intrinsic carries the long run).
- Quarterly: "Does this feel like part of who you are now? 1–7" (Caldwell's four-item scale, single-item proxy). Store as `memories.kind = 'identity'` only when the user says it; the coach reflects the record ("three months of Tuesday runs"), never declares ("you're a runner").
- Quarterly: "Who else knows, or does it with you?" and "Anything changing at home, work, or where you live in the next three months?" (Wing & Jeffery; HS §5).

**Rules and thresholds.**
1. *Satisfaction rule.* Adherence ≥ 80% and satisfaction ≤ 5 two months running → the goal is at maintenance risk whatever the tap counts say. Options in order: name benefits already in the record the user has not counted; renegotiate the outcome expectation; change the form of the behaviour; retire the goal deliberately with a maintenance floor. Never answer low satisfaction with a bigger dose.
2. *Recovery-is-rare rule.* Phelan 2003: once an established behaviour slides, full recovery is the exception. For an action past the gate, two consecutive weeks below the user's own rate, or an outcome metric moving the wrong way for two reviews, triggers a diagnosis conversation now, not at the next monthly check. Shrink to the floor rather than pause: pausing an established behaviour removes the cue (Thomas: regain tracks the *decline* in the deliberate behaviours).
3. *Keep the monitoring, shrink the dose.* Murray 2017: outcome self-monitoring is the maintenance technique with the largest coefficient; Thomas 2014: maintainers who stopped self-weighing regained. Adler's outcome check (GAS or a domain metric) stays weekly through maintenance even when daily taps fade (HS §7). Same days on weekends as weekdays (Wing & Phelan); no "weekend off" offers.
4. *Value link once.* At the gate, one line tying the action to the value the user named at intake (Verplanken & Sui: value affirmation raised habit–identity integration, d = 0.21). Once, not weekly.
5. *No standing incentives.* Hunter 2018: a points scheme reduced steps at six months. Return rewards after a miss (LR) are a different mechanism and stay optional.
6. *Strugglers use the most strategies.* Ogden's second cluster carried the most tools and the most stress. When a maintenance-phase user asks for more techniques, the coach removes one before adding one, and checks resources (WHO-5, CF §6).
7. *Expect the tail.* Ten percent a year is the long-run relapse rate in the best-measured domain, and 29%/54% at one and two years is the tail for a mood goal after therapy-style gains (Vittengl 2007). The Phase 8 relapse plan ("if this slips in a few months, the first small thing you'll do") is rewritten at each quarterly check, not once at close.
8. *Contact, not curriculum.* Cochrane 2019 is the decisive result: across 81 trials and 69,094 people, adding maintenance/relapse-prevention **content** to people who had already changed did nothing, while Middleton, Perri, Svetkey, Voils and Vittengl show continued **contact** does. Adler's maintenance transition is therefore a *schedule* change — shorter, less frequent, same relationship — and not a new module, curriculum, or "maintenance mode" content pack. If a maintenance feature is built, it must be delivered inside the conversation that already exists.
9. *Beat the monitoring floor, not nothing.* NoHoW's active control (generic content + tracker + regular self-weighing) matched all three content arms at 12 months in 1,627 people. So Adler's minimum viable maintenance product is the monitoring loop itself, and the internal comparison for any new maintenance feature is "feature versus keep tracking and check in", never "feature versus no app". Any A/B that lacks a monitoring-only arm cannot answer the question.

**Adapting to one person.** Kwasnicka's N-of-1 shows which theme predicts adherence differs by person; Baldwin 2009 shows satisfaction covaries within-person more than between. Log monthly per goal: satisfaction, effort, enjoyment, identity item, environment stability, resources (WHO-5, "no energy" weeks). When a slide happens, the coach reads which of the five moved first and writes that to the playbook as the user's *maintenance signature* ("slides follow travel", "slides follow satisfaction drops", "slides follow stress weeks"). The registry clusters are a prior for the first three months only: a first-attempt user who reports low effort early needs monitoring and nothing else; a lifelong-struggler profile (many prior attempts, high stress) needs the resource questions ahead of the technique questions. After two logged slides, the user's own signature outranks the prior.

**Avoid.** "Graduating" a goal to zero contact; shipping a "maintenance programme" of new content at the gate (Cochrane 2019); calling digital maintenance features evidence-based (NoHoW, NULevel and the Svetkey technology arm are three nulls); automated weekly summaries as the only maintenance touch; promising that it becomes effortless (it becomes cheaper); identity badges, "you're a runner now", or any identity declaration; satisfaction questions in the first eight weeks (nothing to be satisfied with yet); standing points or rewards; weekend exemptions; pausing an established action when shrinking would do; adding a technique to a user already carrying several; treating a regained slide as recoverable by willpower.

## Conflicts and open questions

1. **Habit makes maintenance effortless vs registry vigilance.** Habit theory and Kwasnicka's third theme say cost falls to near zero; Thomas 2014 shows ten years of self-weighing and restraint with regain when they stop. Evidence favours the split reading: instigation automates, monitoring stays deliberate and light, effort declines (Klem) without vanishing. Copy should say "cheaper", never "automatic".
2. **Satisfaction vs pleasure.** Klem 2000 found pleasure unrelated to maintenance duration; Rothman's line finds satisfaction predicts maintenance; Teixeira finds intrinsic motivation predicts long-term adherence. Not a contradiction: satisfaction is about outcomes, enjoyment about the act, and Klem's null is cross-sectional duration. Ask both; expect them to move separately.
3. **Identity direction.** Porter 2024 (identity → behaviour, not the reverse) vs Gillman 2021 and Rhodes 2025 (behaviour changes identity, modestly) vs AH's audit (action → identity better supported than the reverse). Both directions have support; the *intervention* evidence for instilling identity is weak (van Vliet: half null; Rhodes 2025: g = 0.18). Favour building identity from the record and reflecting it.
4. **Fjeldsoe's 72% vs the decay meta-analyses.** Fjeldsoe counts trials with any significant between-group difference at follow-up among the minority that measured it; Murray and Mönninghoff pool effect sizes. Favour the pooled decay.
5. **Digital maintenance works (Mönninghoff, 8 long-term studies, 80% high risk of bias) vs NULevel, Svetkey's technology arm and now NoHoW (N = 1,627, null against an active control).** Three RCT nulls against one pooled estimate drawn from eight high-risk-of-bias studies. Favour the nulls for *automated content and feedback*. Whether an AI conversation counts as "personal contact" in Svetkey's and Perri's sense is untested and is Adler's central maintenance hypothesis; the cohort retention metrics (CF §6) at weeks 26 and 52 are the test, and the honest null hypothesis is NoHoW's.
6. **Extended care helps (Middleton g = 0.385; Perri 1.2 vs 3.7 kg; Svetkey −1.5 kg) vs contact frequency not mattering (Hartmann-Boyce 2014: −0.3 kg, ns) vs "some support beats extended support" (Oscarsson, LR §3).** Three different constructs. Hartmann-Boyce varied contact *within* an initiation programme; Middleton and Perri added contact *after* one; Oscarsson's "extended" was more content at the start. Evidence favours: extra contact during initiation is not worth much, continued contact after it is the single most replicated maintenance ingredient.
7. **Maintenance content: Cochrane's 81-trial null vs Voils's positive RCT.** Voils delivered satisfaction-focused maintenance content *by telephone*, i.e. content plus contact, and beat a no-contact usual-care arm; Cochrane's abstainer-randomised trials mostly compared content-plus-contact against other contact and found nothing. Evidence favours attributing the effect to the contact and the monitoring, not the maintenance-specific content; Voils remains worth copying for its *delivery*, not its curriculum.
8. **Incentives.** Hunter 2018 negative, Preuhs 2026 positive for long-term weight (BCT §2), Milkman's return reward positive (LR). Favour: no standing incentive; a one-off return reward is a separate mechanism.
9. **Generalisability.** Nearly all maintenance evidence is weight, activity and smoking in clinical, older or overweight samples; identity evidence is students and adolescents. The nearest to 16–40 optimisers are Norcross 2002 and ten Broeke & Adriaanse 2026 (a preprint). Effect sizes here are priors to be replaced by Adler's own data, not claims to put in copy.
10. **Definition.** No agreed definition of maintenance exists (Murray 2018; Rhodes & Sui 2021). Adler's gate (SRBAI ≥ 5, ≥ 80% for 8 weeks, effort falling) is a working rule to be validated against 26- and 52-week adherence.
11. **Open.** Whether monthly satisfaction and effort items predict a slide four weeks ahead in a coaching-app cohort; whether the maintenance signature stabilises after two slides; whether identity reflected from the record moves the identity item at all; and whether a conversational agent clears the bar that NoHoW's toolkit did not, against a monitoring-only control.
