# Social support, accountability, commitment devices

Research date: 2026-09-02. Companion to `coaching-framework.md` (CF §n), `deep/motivation-science.md` (MS §n), `deep/self-regulation-theory.md` (SRT §n), `deep/bct-effectiveness.md` (BCT §n), `deep/individual-differences.md` (ID §n), `deep/adaptive-interventions.md` (ADI §n), `deep/maintenance-long-term.md` (ML §n). Established there and not repeated: Harkin 2016 headline d = 0.40 and Gollwitzer 2009 public *identity* intentions (CF §1); Coupe 2019 and Volpp 2008 (CF §1 rank 12); Bryan, Karlan & Nelson 2010, Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002, Kaur 2015, Halpern 2015 acceptance rates (MS §5); the Duckworth 2 × 2 (SRT §4); Mohr 2011 supportive accountability (ADI §5); Eagle 2023 body doubling and Bond & Titus (ID §2); social support as a maintenance ingredient (ML §6); BCT 3.x associations (BCT §7).

Evidence levels: **A** meta-analysis or multi-site RCT; **B** single RCT or field experiment; **C** correlational or cohort; **D** qualitative, uncontrolled survey, or theory.

## 0. Four mechanisms that get called "accountability"

The literature conflates four things with different effect signs; separating them is this file's contribution. **Witnessing** — someone will see whether you did it, with no help and no evaluation. **Support** — someone helps. **Comparison** — someone else's performance is visible next to yours. **Stakes** — a self-imposed cost for missing. Witnessing has the cleanest experimental evidence and the best effect per unit of cost. Support works in some domains, is null in others, and backfires when made visible. Comparison beats support head-to-head and harms whoever is behind. Stakes work for the minority who accept them and are refused by most.

---

## 1. Witnessed self-monitoring: the strongest result here

Harkin et al. 2016 (*Psychological Bulletin*; **A**, 138 RCTs, N = 19,951) coded every trial for whether monitoring was public, private-but-reported, or private-and-unreported. Goal-attainment subgroups:

- Public monitoring: d+ = 0.55, 95% CI [−0.16, 1.26], **k = 3**, N = 214.
- Private, reported to someone: d+ = 0.47 [0.37, 0.58], k = 95, N = 12,155.
- Private, not reported: d+ = 0.19 [0.05, 0.33], k = 14, N = 3,177.
- Between-groups Q = 49.86, p < .001. Recorded vs not recorded: 0.43 [0.34, 0.53] (k = 106) vs 0.29 [0.15, 0.42] (k = 32), Q = 12.71, p < .001.

Three consequences. The "report it to someone" boost is roughly a **2.5× multiplier** on the monitoring effect, estimated from 95 trials. The *public* subgroup — what habit apps imitate with feeds and leaderboards — rests on three studies with a CI spanning zero. And in these trials the recipient is usually a researcher, clinician or class leader, not a friend: the active ingredient is a designated recipient who will look, not intimacy and not an audience. Harkin's discussion names the rival explanations honestly — public commitment, accountability, self-presentation, experimenter demand — which these data cannot separate.

Guided digital interventions agree. Karyotaki et al. 2021 (*JAMA Psychiatry*; **A**, IPD network meta-analysis, 39 RCTs, 9,751 patients): guided iCBT beat unguided by MD = −0.8 PHQ-9 [−1.4, −0.2], with adherence 76% vs 54%; the advantage concentrated at moderate-to-severe baseline severity, and at subthreshold severity unguided was equivalent. Guidance in most trials was brief scheduled paraprofessional contact — a witness with a calendar, not therapy.

## 2. Social support: real, domain-dependent, null when the support never materialises

**Positive.** CPSTF (2001; **A**, 9 studies) recommends community social-support interventions on strong evidence: median +44.2% time physically active (IQI 19.9–45.6), +19.6% frequency, +4.7% aerobic capacity. Wing & Jeffery 1999 (*JCCP*; **B**, N = 166) randomised recruitment *with three friends* crossed with a support package: 66% of the friends-plus-support group held their full weight loss from months 4 to 10 versus 24% recruited alone with standard treatment (completion 95% vs 76%). Jensen et al. 2024 (*Int J Obesity*; **A**, 24 RCTs, 4,919 adults): couple- and peer-based weight-loss interventions null at 2–4 and 6 months, significant at end of intervention and 3- and 6-month follow-up — with effect reporting so poor (CIs without point estimates) that it is itself a quality flag.

**Null.** Faseru et al. 2018 (*Cochrane*; **A**; 12 studies / 2,818 at 6–9 months, 7 / 2,573 at 12 months): enhancing partner support did not increase quitting, and most trials failed to show the intervention increased partner support at all. The mechanism was never delivered.

**Failed replication.** Long et al. 2012 (*Annals*; **B**, N = 118) found peer mentoring cut HbA1c 1.07 points versus control over six months. Long et al. 2020 (*JAMA Netw Open*; **B**, N = 365), with a one-hour mentor curriculum, found −0.52% vs −0.20%, p = .06, and nothing in phase two. Evidence favours the null: the larger trial failed. Peer support is a skilled role, not a pairing.

**Support hurts when visible.** Bolger & Amarel 2007 (*JPSP*; **B**, three experiments): invisible support reduced emotional reactivity to a stressor while visible support *increased* distress, by signalling the recipient's inadequacy (Zee & Bolger 2019 review the moderators). Help delivered as an adjusted plan beats help announced as help. Fitzsimons, Finkel & vanDellen 2015 (*Psychological Review*; **D**) supply the vocabulary — interdependent pairs as one self-regulating system — but no intervention evidence.

## 3. Comparison beats support head-to-head, and that is not good news

Zhang, Brackbill, Yang & Centola 2016 (*Preventive Medicine Reports*; **B**, four-arm RCT, N = 790, 13 weeks): mean weekly exercise-class attendance 35.7 (comparison), 38.5 (combined), 20.3 (control), **16.8 (social support)**. Comparison arms ran ~90% above the non-comparison arms; the supportive-team arm finished below control. The authors' reading: supportive groups make the least active member visible and start a downward spiral.

Patel et al. 2019 (STEP UP, *JAMA Intern Med*; **A**, N = 602, 24 weeks + 12-week follow-up), where "support" meant a self-chosen family member or friend receiving weekly reports: support +689 daily steps vs control [267, 977], collaboration +637 [258, 1017], competition +920 [513, 1328]. After gamification was switched off: support +428 [19, 837], collaboration +126 (ns), competition +569 [142, 996]. A named private witness kept about 60% of its effect post-programme. Patel et al. 2016 (*Am J Health Promot*; **B**, N = 286) shows comparison is dose-sensitive: feedback against the **50th** percentile plus incentives worked; against the 75th it did not. On the harm side (**C**), survey work (N = 1,452 fitness-app users) finds upward comparison mediating app use → wellbeing and reversing sign for low-self-control users, with higher-BMI users disengaging; Dishion, McCord & Poulin 1999 (*American Psychologist*) warns that aggregating people around a shared problem can be iatrogenic. Competition is thus the most effective social lever and the most dangerous one for optimizers who already over-compare — and the easiest for Adler to avoid, having no social graph.

## 4. Group versus individual format: format is not the ingredient

Kritz et al. 2026 (*Nature Human Behaviour*; **A**, 71 studies, 523 effect sizes) found no advantage either way: g = 0.086 [−0.061, 0.233], p = .249 for physical activity (n = 22,042); g = 0.292 [−0.171, 0.755], p = .214 for psychosocial outcomes (n = 18,223). It supersedes Burke et al. 2006 (**A**, 44 studies, 214 effect sizes), whose ladder — true groups > standard classes ≈ home-with-contact > home-without-contact — was driven by *contact*, not format. Paul-Ebhohimhen & Avenell 2009 (**A**, 5 trials, 336), Renjilian et al. 2001 (**B**, N = 75; group beat individual regardless of stated preference) and Losch et al. 2016 (**B**, N = 84; individual coaching and group training both cut procrastination) agree. Jebb et al. 2011 (*Lancet*; **A**, N = 772) shows what a group programme with a weekly witnessed weigh-in achieves at scale: −5.06 kg vs −2.25 kg standard care at 12 months (adjusted difference −2.77 kg [−3.50, −2.03]). Kullgren et al. 2013 (**B**, N = 105) adds that pooling *incentives* across a group produced 10.6 lb versus 3.7 lb for individual incentives — the shared money, not the meeting. The null on format is permission not to build groups; the contact gradient is the part worth copying, and one-to-one contact is what Adler has.

## 5. Commitment devices and deposits

Boonmanunt et al. 2023 (*Annals of Behavioral Medicine*; **A**, network meta-analysis, 35 RCTs) is the best current synthesis. Versus no incentive at programme end: deposits RR 1.79 [1.04, 3.05] for diet/weight and 1.63 [1.24, 2.14] for physical activity; standard incentives 1.21 (ns) and 1.38 [1.13, 1.68]; lotteries 1.45 (ns) and 1.43 [1.14, 1.80]. At 2-week-to-6-month follow-up **only deposits held**: RR 1.39 [1.11, 1.73] against 1.11 and 1.05 (both ns). Deposits ranked first on SUCRA for both outcomes; the authors flag imprecision (about half the estimates at moderate confidence) and uptake as the binding constraint.

Uptake is the recurring finding: de Buisonjé et al. 2022 (*JMIR*; **B**) found deposit uptake 29/47 (62%) versus 50/50 (100%) for rewards, with deposits **not** more effective than equivalent rewards — alongside Halpern 2015's 13.7% acceptance (MS §5). Rogers, Milkman & Volpp 2014 (*JAMA* viewpoint; **D**) state the two defining features — voluntary adoption and self-imposed consequences for failure — and argue devices are underused; the published reply raises the risk that penalties land on people failing for reasons outside their control.

**Audit of the popular numbers.** stickK's own analysis of ~125,000 contracts reports success rising from 29% (no referee, no stakes) to 59% (referee) to ~80% (both); a separate analysis of 17,654 contracts found under a third of users chose monetary stakes. **D**-grade — self-selected users, self-reported outcomes, no randomisation, and the referee condition confounds witnessing with stakes — but the lever it points at is Harkin's, not the money's. The "ASTD: 65% with a commitment, 95% with an accountability appointment" statistic saturating accountability-app marketing has no traceable study behind it, and Matthews' Dominican University goal study (N = 267; ~70% of weekly reporters succeeded vs 35% of private goal-setters) is a conference presentation, not a peer-reviewed paper. Neither belongs in Adler's copy.

## 6. Can a machine be the witness?

Terblanche et al. 2022 (*PLOS ONE*; **B**; two matched 10-month RCTs, 327 analysed: human coach n = 105 + control 105; AI chatbot n = 134 + control 134): both coaching arms beat controls on goal attainment (human η²p = .265, AI η²p = .269, p < .001) and never differed from each other. Terblanche et al. 2026 (*Human Resource Development International*; **B**) did not reproduce the goal-attainment result, finding a significant AI effect only on general self-efficacy. Honest summary: one positive trial in undergraduates with self-reported outcomes, plus a partial non-replication. Combined with Harkin (the witness is usually impersonal) and Karyotaki (guidance mainly buys adherence), the defensible position is that **a designated, non-judging recipient of a daily report is very likely deliverable by software**; the claim that a specific human's disappointment is required is unevidenced.

**Body doubling and co-working** remain untested (ID §2). New since: a VR pilot comparing solo work with human and AI body doubles (N = 12, within-subject) reported 27–30% faster completion, and an exploratory EEG study in ADHD (ASSETS 2025). Focusmate's February 2024 survey of 834 active members (99% say it improves productivity, mean self-reported +143%, 100% of members with diagnosed ADHD) is **D**-grade: retained users, no control, no objective outcome, ceiling agreement rates that are themselves a demand signal. Given the commercial scale of virtual co-working, the absence of a controlled trial is the largest evidence gap in this file.

## 7. Loneliness and goal pursuit

WHO Commission on Social Connection 2025: about 1 in 6 people report loneliness, highest in adolescents (20.9% of 13–17s) and young adults (17.4% of 18–29s) — Adler's exact demographic — with ~871,000 associated deaths a year. Holt-Lunstad et al. 2015 (**A**, 70 studies, ~3.4 million): adjusted mortality OR 1.29 social isolation, 1.26 loneliness, 1.32 living alone, and larger in samples under 65. Mechanism: Baumeister, DeWall, Ciarocco & Twenge 2005 (*JPSP*; **B**, six experiments) showed social exclusion impairs self-regulation — quitting sooner on frustrating tasks, eating more, worse attention control — and that a cash incentive or a mirror **eliminated** the decrement. Excluded people can self-regulate; they stop wanting to. That maps onto a user who goes quiet and taps "didn't feel like it". Interventions do less than the problem's size implies: Lasgaard et al. 2025 (*American Psychologist*; **A**, 280 studies, >30,000 participants) found small-to-moderate reductions, best for psychological rather than contact-arranging interventions; Osborn et al. 2024 (**A**, young adults) found depression SMD −0.19 but loneliness SMD −0.10 [−0.24, 0.05], ns.

## Implications for Adler

**Core claim.** Position the daily check-in as *witnessed private self-monitoring* — the d = 0.47 cell — not a public feed (k = 3, CI spans zero) and not a companion relationship (D9). The witness role: a designated recipient who looks, notices absence, does not evaluate the person, and does not praise compliance. Compatible with D3 and D5; no new surface required.

**Questions to ask** (intake and review, optional, behavioural, stored as parameters not judgements).
- "Does anyone else currently know what you're working on?"
- "When you've kept something going before, was anyone involved? What were they doing?" (distinguishes witness / helper / competitor / co-worker in the user's own history)
- "If you skipped three days, would you rather I said nothing, asked what happened, or just showed the plan again?" (sets the witness contract; ICF 8.7 — the client designs their own accountability method)
- Only if the user raises stakes: "Do you want teeth on this, or is telling me enough?"
- Reuse, don't duplicate: "Is it easier to start when someone else is around or expecting you?" (ID §Implications).

**Rules and thresholds** (hypotheses on evidence, each logged and checked per D3).
1. *Witness, never audience.* No feeds, leaderboards, follower counts, shared streaks or percentile ranks against other users. Competition works (Zhang/Centola, STEP UP); its harm profile in an over-comparing 16–40 audience, plus the EU AI Act line on exploiting vulnerability (CF §5), puts it out of scope.
2. *Name the recipient in the copy.* "Recorded" and "got it" beat silence; the mechanism is a recipient, not a database.
3. *Notice absence.* The one thing a witness does that a log cannot. Re-engage at 3+ days per D3, max two messages, never "I missed you".
4. *Invisible support first.* Deliver help as an adjusted plan ("shall we make it the two-minute version this week?"), never as announced assistance that narrates the user's deficiency (Bolger & Amarel).
5. *Human witnesses are the user's to recruit, never Adler's to assign.* If a partner or friend is already involved, help design what gets reported (actions done, CF §1 rank 13) and when. Build no matching or buddy features: untrained peer support failed (Long 2020, Faseru 2018).
6. *Stakes are opt-in, user-initiated, capped, reversible.* Deposits are the only incentive that persists (Boonmanunt) and most people refuse them (de Buisonjé 38%, Halpern 86%). Never offer unprompted; prefer soft stakes (tell a named person, forfeit a slot) before money; record refusal as incentive preference, not low commitment (MS §5).
7. *No coach-imposed penalties.* Penalties land hardest on non-volitional misses, and this keeps Adler inside NBHWC scope ("accountability partner, not director").
8. *Comparison is intrapersonal only* — the user against their own last four weeks. If a user demands external comparison, Patel 2016 says make the target attainable (50th, not 75th percentile); the default answer is still no.
9. *Loneliness is a flag, not a target.* "Nobody knows what I'm doing" + global adherence drop + "didn't feel like it" dominance is the Baumeister signature (motivation withdrawn, capacity intact): shrink the action, make the immediate result visible, and surface once, without pressure, that a person may help more than an app. Never sell Adler as a cure for loneliness (D9).
10. *Do not claim body doubling.* Name co-working as an option a user might try for initiation barriers (ID §2); never describe Adler's own presence as one.

**Adapting to one person over time.** Four parameters are estimable from the existing tap stream with no new instrument: *witness responsiveness* (adherence in the 7 days after an acknowledged vs unacknowledged check-in); *report latency* (delay between scheduled check-in and answer — rising latency is the disengagement signal in the guided/unguided literature); *social initiation dependence* (adherence on actions done with someone vs alone); *stakes appetite* (whether consequences are ever raised unprompted). Confirmed values become playbook entries — "this user needs the report acknowledged", "this user's adherence is indifferent to whether anyone knows" — and change the coach's phrasing. That is the per-user version of the Harkin moderator.

**Avoid.** Leaderboards, feeds, shared streaks, percentile ranks, buddy matching, coach-imposed penalties, announced help, any implication of the coach's feelings, guilt as a lever, the ASTD 65/95% and Matthews 42% statistics in copy, describing Adler as a friend or a body double, and claiming that "accountability" is what makes goals work when the evidence says it is *the record plus a recipient*.

## Conflicts and open questions

- **Support helps (CPSTF, Wing & Jeffery, Jensen) vs does nothing (Faseru) vs is worse than nothing (Zhang/Centola).** Best-fitting reading: support helps when *enacted and skilled* (Wing & Jeffery's trained package, Jebb's group leaders) and fails or backfires when merely *arranged* (assign a buddy, form a team). Faseru's finding that the trials did not even raise partner support is the strongest evidence for it. Favours: strengthen the one relationship that already exists rather than arranging new ties.
- **Competition wins on effect size, loses on ethics.** STEP UP and Zhang/Centola both favour competition, including on durability; the harm evidence is correlational. Adler's refusal is a values decision, stated here rather than hidden — the clearest place where Adler leaves measurable effect on the table.
- **Harkin's "reported" cell cannot separate accountability from experimenter demand.** The authors say so. If demand drives it, an app's request may be a weaker manipulation than a researcher's. Testable in Adler's own logs (acknowledged vs unacknowledged check-ins).
- **Does an AI witness reproduce the human-witness effect?** Terblanche 2022 says AI ≈ human; Terblanche 2026 fails to replicate the goal part; Karyotaki's guidance effect is small in symptoms but large in adherence. No trial isolates "reported to a machine" vs "to a person" vs "recorded privately". The one experiment Adler is best placed to run.
- **Deposits are the best-performing and least-accepted device.** Both can be true (the people who accept are the people it works for). Unresolved: whether offering a deposit and having it refused damages the alliance.
- **Group-format nulls (Kritz 2026) vs older group superiority (Burke, Renjilian, Paul-Ebhohimhen).** Kritz is larger and newer; evidence favours the null on format. Neither settles whether cohesion helps, because cohesion is rarely manipulated.
- **Body doubling.** Still no controlled trial three years after Eagle 2023 said so; the commercial evidence is unusable.
- **Loneliness → goal pursuit is causal in the lab and correlational in the field.** Baumeister manipulates acute exclusion, not chronic loneliness. Extrapolation, flagged.
- **Audience transfer.** Nearly all accountability and incentive evidence comes from clinical or employee samples with health outcomes and low baseline motivation; Adler's users are self-selected high-motivation optimizers with heterogeneous goals. Ceiling effects and different comparison sensitivity are plausible and untested.
