# Emotion regulation, wellbeing, burnout and recovery: foundations for Adler

## 1. Emotion regulation: the process model, what works, and the flexibility question

**Model (D).** Gross 1998, 2015: emotions unfold situation → attention → appraisal → response, and regulation can intervene at each stage (situation selection/modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change, response modulation). The 2015 extended model adds three regulation stages (identify, select, implement). Heavily cited; still a framework, not a falsifiable prediction.

**Which strategies work (A).** Webb, Miles & Sheeran 2012 (*Psych Bull*; 306 experimental comparisons): attentional deployment d+ = 0.00 overall, but distraction 0.27 and concentration on the feeling −0.26; cognitive change 0.36, split into perspective-taking 0.45, reappraising the stimulus 0.36, reappraising one's own response 0.23; response modulation 0.16, split into suppressing expression 0.32 but suppressing the *experience* −0.04 and suppressing thoughts −0.12. Practical reading: change the meaning of the situation, or take another's view; do not try to not feel.

**Dispositional strategies and mental health (A, correlational).** Aldao, Nolen-Hoeksema & Schweizer 2010 (114 studies, 241 effects): rumination large; avoidance/suppression/problem-solving medium-to-large; reappraisal and acceptance only small-to-medium. Hu et al. 2014 (51 samples, N = 21,150): reappraisal r = .26 with positive and −.20 with negative mental health; suppression −.12/.15, weaker in Eastern samples. Maladaptive strategies predict symptoms more strongly than adaptive ones protect — the asymmetry matters for a coach: removing rumination beats adding reappraisal.

**Acceptance (B/C).** Ford, Lam, John & Mauss 2018 (*JPSP*): habitual acceptance of mental experiences predicted psychological health controlling for reappraisal and rumination (N = 1,003), lower negative response to a lab stressor (N = 156), and 6-month psychological health via lower daily negative emotion (N = 222). Accepting *situations* did not carry the effect; accepting *feelings about* them did.

**Strategy choice depends on intensity (B, lab).** Sheppes, Scheibe, Suri & Gross 2011 (*Psych Sci*, three experiments; pictures and unpredictable electric stimulation): in low-intensity contexts people prefer engagement reappraisal; in high-intensity contexts they prefer disengagement distraction. Replicated and extended in clinical and non-clinical samples (e.g. Collabra 2023 replication). This is about *choice*, not about outcomes — no trial shows that being pushed toward the intensity-matched strategy improves wellbeing.

**Does "fit" predict wellbeing? Probably not, at the person level (A-adjacent replication evidence).** Troy, Shallcross & Mauss 2013 (*Psych Sci*, N = 170; corrigendum 2016, 27(3):428–31): high reappraisal ability went with *lower* depression under uncontrollable stress and *higher* depression under controllable stress. Haines et al. 2016 (*Psych Sci*, n = 74, EMA 10×/day for a week): people whose reappraisal use tracked perceived uncontrollability had higher wellbeing; mean reappraisal use did not. **Willroth, Young, Ford, Troy, Swierzewicz & Mauss 2025** (*Psych Sci* 36(5):367–383; preregistered direct replication, N = 285): failed to replicate, "no evidence for the strategy-situation fit hypothesis", including with confounders and moderators. Verdict: keep the *clinical* heuristic (if you can change the situation, change it — SR §3 already ranks situational over cognitive), drop any plan to score a user's regulation "fit".

**Emotion differentiation (A, correlational).** Seah & Coifman 2022 (*Emotion* 22(7):1686–97; 17 studies): negative emotion differentiation correlated r = −.15 with maladaptive behaviours (binge drinking, NSSI, treatment non-compliance), identical in clinical (−.15) and non-clinical (−.16) samples, and surviving control for negative affect (r = −.09, k = 11). O'Toole et al. 2020 finds a small positive relation with behavioural adaptation. Small, but it is the empirical basis for asking for *one specific word*, not "how are you feeling?".

**Affect labelling (B, lab; contested).** Lieberman et al. 2007 (fMRI, N = 30) and Torre & Lieberman 2018: naming a feeling reduces amygdala response and, in some studies, self-reported distress, without feeling like regulation. Torre & Lieberman themselves note self-report effects are inconsistent and sometimes null or negative in the short term. Cheap; do not promise it works.

**Mood repair and procrastination (D + B).** Sirois & Pychyl 2013: procrastination is short-term mood repair at the future self's expense. Eckert et al. 2016 (cross-lagged panel plus RCT): training *tolerate* and *modify* aversive-emotion skills reduced procrastination. Consistent with LR and SR: "didn't feel like it" is an emotion-regulation event, not a motivation deficit.

## 2. Affect as the leading indicator of habit maintenance

- Rhodes, Fiala & Conner 2009 (A; 102 studies, 114 samples): affective judgements ("enjoyable / pleasant") correlate r = .42 with physical activity, invariant across measures and populations. Persuasive, informational and self-regulatory interventions **failed to change** affective judgements; environmental and experiential ones did.
- Rhodes & Kates 2015 (A; systematic review, 24 studies, *Ann Behav Med* 49:715–31): affective response *during* moderate-intensity exercise predicted future activity; **post-exercise affect had a null relation**; affect had a negligible relation to intention and a reliable relation to affective judgements. Ekkekakis, Parfitt & Petruzzello 2011: below the ventilatory threshold responses are homogeneously pleasant, above it mostly unpleasant, near it individually variable. Dose determines feeling.
- Liao, Shonkoff & Dunton 2015 (C; 14 EMA studies): positive affect predicts activity in the next hours; negative affect does not; activity raises positive affect and energy afterwards.
- Conner et al. 2015 (A + C; k = 16; N = 426): affective attitude predicts intention and behaviour beyond TPB variables and past behaviour; *anticipated* affective reactions are weaker and inconsistent.
- Brewer et al. 2016 (A; 81 studies, N = 45,618): anticipated regret r = .50 with intentions, .29 with behaviour; **inaction** regret is the stronger, more stable predictor. A real lever, and a guilt lever; CF's don'ts still apply.
- Van Cappellen et al. 2018 (D with C support): upward-spiral theory — positive affect during a behaviour raises incentive salience of its cues and drives non-conscious repetition. Fits Judah 2018 (HS §2).

**Verdict.** The affect that predicts maintenance is felt *during* the act, not afterwards and not in the forecast. It changes by making the act pleasant or easier (dose, setting, company), not by talking about it. This is the single most product-relevant finding in this document.

## 3. Self-affirmation

Cohen & Sherman 2014 (*Annu Rev Psychol*): writing about core values buffers threats to self-integrity, reduces defensiveness, can start a "cycle of adaptive potential". Epton et al. 2015 (A; 144 tests): message acceptance d+ = .17, intentions .14, behaviour .32 (N = 2,715). Sweeney & Moyer 2015 (A; 16 studies): intentions .26, behaviour .27; intention effects did not predict behaviour effects. Hanselman et al. 2017 (B; preregistered large-scale replication in a setting with a prior positive trial): null, precise enough to reject effects above 0.10; theorised moderators did not explain the gap. Kizilcec et al. 2020 (*PNAS*; 250,000 learners, 247 courses): value-relevance affirmations raised completion only where a global achievement gap existed. **Verdict:** small, fragile, threat-specific — it helps a person hear bad news without shutting down. Not a motivator; the behaviour data are health messages, not goal pursuit.

## 4. Mindfulness: effects, comparators, harms

- Goyal et al. 2014 (A; 47 active-controlled trials, N = 3,515): anxiety d = 0.38 at 8 weeks, 0.22 at 3–6 months; depression 0.30/0.23; pain 0.33; low or insufficient evidence for positive mood, attention, sleep, weight; **no evidence that meditation beat any active treatment**.
- Goldberg et al. 2018 (A; 142 samples, N = 12,005, clinical): vs no treatment d = 0.55; minimal treatment 0.37; non-specific active 0.35; specific active 0.23; evidence-based treatment −0.004. Goldberg et al. 2022 umbrella review (44 meta-analyses, 336 RCTs, N = 30,483): healthy adults' wellbeing d = 0.21 (k = 7); stress 0.40 passive / 0.18 active; employees 0.43 / 0.22; app-delivered 0.54 passive, non-significant vs active and at follow-up.
- Apps: Gál, Ștefan & Cristea 2021 (A; 34 RCTs, N = 7,566): stress g = 0.46, anxiety 0.28, depression 0.33, wellbeing 0.29. Linardon 2020 (27 RCTs): mindfulness/acceptance g = 0.29, self-compassion 0.31; only 18% low risk of bias; "likely explained by a digital placebo". Linardon et al. 2023 (45 RCTs, N ≈ 6,000): depression g = 0.24, anxiety 0.28; no better than active therapeutic comparisons.
- Self-regulation specifically: Leyland, Rowse & Emerson 2019 (A; 27 studies): a single induction improved regulation of induced negative affect d = −0.28 (k = 15); executive-function gains only after an affect induction or on sustained attention; low study quality. Mechanism is attention, not cognitive change.
- Critique (D): Van Dam et al. 2018, "Mind the Hype" (*Perspect Psychol Sci* 13(1):36–61), by 15 mostly contemplative scientists, on definitional incoherence, weak measurement and public over-claiming.
- Harm (B/C): Farias et al. 2020 (83 studies, N = 6,703): adverse events 8.3% overall (3.7% experimental, 33% observational). **Britton et al. 2021** (*Clin Psych Sci*; structured 44-item interview, n = 96 across three MBCT variants): 83% reported at least one meditation-related side effect, 58% with negative valence, 37% with negative impact on functioning, **6–14% lasting bad effects**. A far higher base rate than trial reporting suggests, because trials rarely ask.

**Verdict.** Real but small effects, largest against doing nothing, near zero against any credible alternative, with a non-trivial adverse-event rate that only shows up when you ask. CF §3 already lists meditation as optional and never prescribed; add: never sell it as productivity or attention training, and always ask how it went.

## 5. Positive psychology interventions, and the gratitude case

Sin & Lyubomirsky 2009 (51 studies): r = .29 wellbeing, .31 depression. Bolier et al. 2013 (39 RCTs, N = 6,139): SMD 0.34 subjective wellbeing, 0.20 psychological wellbeing, 0.23 depression, with publication bias. White, Uttl & Holder 2019 (re-analysis): after small-sample bias correction, wellbeing r ≈ .10 and depression not significant. Carr et al. 2021 (A; 347 studies, >72,000 participants, 41 countries): wellbeing g = 0.39, strengths 0.46, quality of life 0.48, depression −0.39, anxiety −0.62, stress −0.58, held at 3 months; largest for longer individual or group programmes in clinical samples. Gorlin, Lee & Otto 2018: future-focused interventions (goal pursuit, optimism training) are more uniformly positive than past/present-focused ones.

Gratitude is the cleanest test case because it is the most-copied app feature. Cregg & Cheavens 2021 (*J Happiness Stud*; k = 27, N = 3,675): modest effects on depression and anxiety, with the authors explicitly recommending better-evidenced methods. **Choi, Cha, McCullough, Coles & Oishi 2025** (*PNAS* 122(28); preregistered; 145 papers, 163 samples, 727 effect sizes, N = 24,804, 28 countries): wellbeing **g = 0.19, 95% CI [0.15, 0.22]**, robust to publication bias; significant between-country variation with no moderator explaining it; larger when the outcome was positive affect and when interventions were combined.

Audit item: Fredrickson & Losada's 2.9013 "critical positivity ratio" was shown to rest on misapplied fluid-dynamics mathematics (Brown, Sokal & Friedman 2013, *Am Psychol* 68:801–813); the modelling element was formally withdrawn, and Nickerson 2018 concludes there is no empirical basis for critical ratios at all. Any product that ships a positivity ratio is shipping a retracted result.

**Verdict.** For the *non-clinical, self-help* case Adler serves, favour White 2019 and Choi 2025 over Carr 2021: g ≈ 0.1–0.2 unless there is a human and a real dose. Adler is already the best-supported PPI type (structured goal pursuit, ρ = .43 progress–wellbeing, §9); bolting on a gratitude journal adds ~0.19 at best and dilutes the product.

## 6. Self-compassion, deeper than CF rank 7

Ferrari et al. 2019 (A; *Mindfulness* 10:1455–1473; 27 RCTs, N = 1,480): significant improvement across 11 psychosocial outcomes; self-compassion **g = 0.75**; positive affect g = 0.48 [0.19, 0.77]; life satisfaction g = 0.40 [0.05, 0.75]; significant changes in stress, depression, self-criticism and anxiety; the headline-grabbing outcomes (eating behaviour, rumination) rest on two or three trials each. Kirby, Tellegen & Steindl 2017 (21 RCTs, N = 1,285): self-compassion d = 0.70, depression 0.64, anxiety 0.49, wellbeing 0.51, **holding against active controls**; small samples. Wilson et al. 2018 (22 RCTs, N ≤ 1,172): g = 0.52 / 0.46 / 0.40 for self-compassion, anxiety, depression, but **not significant against active controls**. Wakelin, Perman & Simonds 2021 (19 RCTs, N = 1,350): self-criticism g = 0.51, larger for longer programmes and passive controls. Phillips & Hine 2021 (A; 94 papers, N = 29,588): self-compassion–health-behaviour r = .26, physical health .18; multi-session interventions moved both; **single-session inductions did nothing**; strongest for sleep, weakest in the 40+ group. Sirois, Nauts & Molnar 2018 (N = 134 and 646, cross-sectional): self-compassion → less bedtime procrastination via lower negative affect, partly via reappraisal.

**Measurement caveat that inflates all of the above.** Muris & Otgaar and colleagues show the Self-Compassion Scale's six subscales split into positively and negatively worded factors; the negative items (self-judgment, isolation, over-identification) carry most of the association with psychopathology, so a total score partly measures self-criticism and tautologically correlates with distress. Neff et al. 2019 defends a bifactor total across 20 samples. Unresolved. Consequence: treat SCS-based effect sizes as upper bounds, and if Adler ever measures self-compassion, score the compassionate items separately.

**Verdict.** A one-line "be kind to yourself" is inert (Phillips & Hine). The active ingredient is repetition. In Adler the repetition is the coach's own stance, weekly, for months (HE's compassionate correction) — and not undoing it with grading or streaks.

## 7. Burnout: construct, classification, overlap, what works

**Construct (D + C).** Maslach & Leiter 2016: exhaustion, cynicism, inefficacy; six mismatches (workload, control, reward, community, fairness, values); five profiles; exhaustion alone is not burnout; most advice targets the individual although situational factors drive it, and "very little research has evaluated the efficacy of any of these approaches".

**Classification (D, authoritative).** ICD-11 lists burn-out as **QD85** under "problems associated with employment or unemployment" — explicitly *not* a medical condition, and defined as resulting from **chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed** (WHO, 28 May 2019). Two consequences for Adler: the label is off-label for personal goals, and using it as if it were a diagnosis is both wrong and, under CF's safety law, out of scope.

**Overlap with depression (A).** Bianchi et al. 2021 (14 samples, N = 12,417): disattenuated exhaustion–depression r = .80, "problematically strong"; exhaustion correlates more with depression than with the other burnout dimensions. Koutsimani et al. 2019: burnout–depression r = .52, anxiety .46, lower with the MBI and in better studies. Maslach and Leiter reply that burnout is job-specific and depression context-free. On the exhaustion core the evidence favours Bianchi: persistent exhaustion is a mental-health signal first.

**Interventions (A).** Maricuțoiu, Sava & Butta 2016 (k = 13–34): burnout d = .22, exhaustion .17, cynicism .04 (ns), accomplishment −.02 (ns), similar at follow-up. Ahola et al. 2017 (4 individually-focused RCTs): no effect on exhaustion or cynicism. Karabinski et al. 2021 (34 interventions, N = 3,725): detachment-focused interventions d = 0.36; longer and higher dose better; more effective for the already-impaired. **Collett et al. 2026** (A; *Ann Intern Med* 179(1):51–66, epub Nov 2025; 93 RCTs + 6 cluster RCTs, N = 9,330 healthcare professionals): **professional coaching** reduced physician emotional exhaustion SMD −0.37 [−0.62, −0.13] (low certainty) and depersonalisation −0.30 [−0.42, −0.19] (moderate certainty), while **mindfulness-based interventions were not effective for physicians** (EE −0.46 [−1.28, 0.35], very low certainty; depersonalisation −0.09 [−0.30, 0.12], moderate certainty); mindfulness did work for nurses and midwives (EE −0.90 [−1.46, −0.34], low certainty) and mixed-role samples (EE −0.40; personal accomplishment 0.48 [0.29, 0.67], moderate certainty). Effective programmes ran **longer than 4 weeks**. This is the most useful new fact in this section: structured coaching is among the better-evidenced individual-level responses to exhaustion, and dose (>4 weeks) is the reliable moderator.

**Who is at risk in Adler's audience (A, correlational).** Hill & Curran 2016 (43 studies, N = 9,838): perfectionistic *concerns* medium-to-large with burnout; *strivings* small and negative; worst in the work domain. Clark et al. 2016: workaholism ties to perfectionism and Type A, is unrelated to conscientiousness or self-esteem, and relates to burnout, stress, work–life conflict and worse health — "best conceptualised as an addiction to work". Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah 2017: heavy work investment r = −.32 with detachment.

**Measurement.** Dolan et al. 2015 (N = 5,404): the non-proprietary single item "Overall, based on your definition of burnout, how would you rate your level of burnout?" (5 levels; ≥ 3 = burning out) correlates .79 with the MBI exhaustion item; sensitivity 83%, specificity 87%, AUC .93. The MBI is licensed; the single item is not.

## 8. Recovery: experiences, doses, and the one strong digital trial

**Constructs.** Sonnentag & Fritz 2007 (REQ, N = 930): psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery, control — four 4-item scales. Sonnentag, Cheng & Parker 2022 (*Annu Rev Organ Psychol Organ Behav* 9:33–60): mechanisms are similar across breaks, evenings, weekends and vacations; recovery *can* be trained.

**Effects (A, mostly correlational).** Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah 2017 (91 samples, N = 38,124): detachment with fatigue r = −.42, exhaustion −.36, wellbeing .32, life satisfaction .32, sleep .30, positive affect .28; task performance .09; engagement .04 (ns); contextual performance −.13 and creativity −.11; work-related activity in non-work time −.31. Bennett, Bakker & Field 2018 (54 samples, N = 26,592): detachment is the strongest recovery experience against fatigue, **control** the strongest for vigour; recovery adds ΔR² = .12 beyond work characteristics. Steed et al. 2021 (198 samples) replicates the direction; magnitudes depend on whether recovery is measured as activities, experiences or states. Fritz et al. 2010: curvilinear — medium detachment best for task and proactive performance. Sonnentag 2018, the **recovery paradox**: recovery processes are impaired exactly when stressors are high.

**Dose forms (A).** de Bloom et al. 2009 (7 studies): vacation d = +0.43, fading to −0.38 within weeks of return. Albulescu et al. 2022 (22 samples, N = 2,335): micro-breaks raise vigour d = .36 and cut fatigue d = .35; performance d = .16 (ns), recovered only with breaks over 10 minutes on depleting tasks. Litwiller et al. 2017 (152 studies): sleep quality and quantity relate negatively to workload and to affective and health strain.

**The digital proof point (B).** Ebert et al. 2015 (*Health Psychol* 34S:1240–51): **unguided**, internet-based, 6-session recovery training vs waitlist in 128 teachers with insomnia (ISI ≥ 15) and work-related rumination. Insomnia severity **d = 1.37 [0.99, 1.77]**; superiority on all secondary outcomes including recovery experiences (the Sonnentag 2022 review reports detachment d = 1.72, relaxation 0.94, mastery 0.67, control 0.76 from this programme); effects mediated by reductions in perseverative cognition and sleep effort; maintained at a naturalistic 6-month follow-up (intervention arm only — uncontrolled, so treat the durability claim as weak). Caveats: waitlist control, selected high-symptom sample, self-report. Still the best existing evidence that a self-serve digital product can move detachment and sleep, and it tells you the mechanism to target: **perseverative cognition, not "relaxation content"**.

**Verdict.** Detachment protects wellbeing and sleep at medium correlational strength, costs a little creativity and discretionary effort, and cannot be added when it is most needed unless demands drop. For a goal coach: reduce the load; do not prescribe rest as one more task.

## 9. Hedonic adaptation and the arrival fallacy

Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman 1978 (N = 22 lottery winners, 29 accident victims): origin of "you adapt to everything" — cross-sectional and tiny. Lindqvist, Östling & Cesarini 2020 (preregistered, Swedish lottery, 5–22 years post-win): sustained life-satisfaction gains with no decay; smaller for happiness and mental health; financial satisfaction mediates. Luhmann et al. 2012 (A; 188 publications, 313 samples, N = 65,911): marriage lifts life satisfaction (b0 = 0.26) then decays (−0.11/yr); childbirth 0.50 then −0.19; unemployment −0.43 with slow, incomplete recovery; adaptation is **not** a function of an event's desirability, and affective and cognitive wellbeing move differently. Sheldon & Lyubomirsky 2012 (N = 481, three waves): the hedonic-adaptation-prevention model — gains erode bottom-up (fewer positive events) and top-down (rising aspirations); continued appreciation and variety slow both. The "40% pie" (Lyubomirsky, Sheldon & Schkade 2005) is critiqued by Brown & Rohrer 2020 as lacking empirical basis.

**The arrival fallacy** (Ben-Shahar's popular term) has three empirical cores. Niemiec, Ryan & Deci 2009 (C; N = 147, one year post-college): attaining *intrinsic* aspirations raised wellbeing via need satisfaction; attaining *extrinsic* ones did not, and related to ill-being. Klug & Maier 2015 (A; 85 studies, 108 samples): goal progress–subjective wellbeing **ρ = .43**, and larger when success is defined as **progress** rather than attainment. Levine et al. 2012 vs Wilson & Gilbert 2013: people forecast feelings *about* an event fairly accurately; they overestimate its effect on their *general* mood. So arrival gives a real but bounded, event-specific lift; the day-to-day payoff sits in progress and in what the goal was for.

## 10. Productivity anxiety and "productivity dysmorphia"

Audit of Codrea-Rado 2021 (*Refinery29*; cited in GD): the phrase traces to a 2020 tweet by designer Ben Uyeda; defined as "an inability to see my own success"; the evidence is Twitter replies and three expert quotes, one (Nagoski) mapping it onto burnout's reduced-accomplishment dimension. No peer-reviewed construct exists (OpenAlex title search "productivity dysmorphia": 0 records). Re-checked 2026-09: still no validated "productivity anxiety" measure; the nearest new instruments are a Work Performance Anxiety item (Lindqvist & Paulsen 2024), a "knowledge anxiety" scale (2024, 16 items, α = .88) and a Future Employment Anxiety Scale (2025) — none validated for this construct or this audience. The validated neighbours remain: reduced personal accomplishment (Maslach), contingent self-worth (GD), perfectionistic concerns (Hill & Curran), workaholism (Clark), the HAP top-down route, and the impact bias. Treat the label as a user's phrase, never a diagnosis.

## Implications for Adler

**Questions to ask.**
- After every "Yes": one tap, "How did it feel *while* you were doing it?" (three faces). This is the leading indicator (Rhodes & Kates); the post-hoc "glad I did it" is explicitly null.
- Before a "No" with barrier *didn't feel like it*: "What was the feeling, in one word?" — one specific word, not "bad" (Seah & Coifman). Then "What would make the 2-minute version *pleasant*, not just possible?" (dose, setting, company; Rhodes 2009: only experience changes affect).
- When the feeling is intense, offer disengagement first ("put it down for tonight"); when it is mild, offer reframing ("what else could this mean?"). Sheppes 2011 is about what people *choose*; use it as a menu ordering, not a prescription, because the outcome version of this idea failed to replicate (Willroth 2025).
- Weekly review, one line: "What's better?" (HE); on a milestone, "What do you want to keep noticing about this?" (appreciation) and "What could you vary next?" (HAP variety). Never "How does it feel to have arrived?".
- Monthly, opt-in, three items: WHO-5 (CF §6), the Dolan single burnout item, and one REQ detachment item ("During time off I forget about work/goals", 1–5).
- Intake: "When you do well, how long does it satisfy you?" Answers like "about a day" or "never" flag the HAP top-down route and contingent worth (GD); log it, do not label it.

**Rules and thresholds.**
- *Affect rule.* Two consecutive weeks where an action's during-affect is mostly negative → treat it as **too hard** even if adherence is fine: cut the dose (below the discomfort threshold), change setting or company, or swap the action. Do not motivate.
- *Anticipated-regret rule.* Use inaction framing sparingly and only in the user's own words ("you said you'd regret not showing up for this"); never coach-generated guilt (CF don'ts).
- *Self-affirmation rule.* One line recalling a value the user stated at intake, immediately **before** a hard summary (≤ 3 of 7 for two weeks, or a proposal to shrink a goal). Not before ordinary check-ins; not as praise.
- *Burnout rule.* Dolan item ≥ 3, or WHO-5 ≤ 50 with detachment ≤ 2 → CF §7 referral script **plus** a load cut: propose pausing every goal but one and keep the coaching dose weekly. Persistent exhaustion routes as a mental-health signal (Bianchi), not "burnout coaching".
- *Language rule.* Never write "burnout" as a state the user is in. ICD-11 defines it as a workplace phenomenon and not a medical condition; say "you're running on empty" and screen.
- *Dose rule.* Anything Adler offers as a wellbeing intervention runs **longer than 4 weeks** or not at all: Collett 2026 (>4 weeks), Phillips & Hine (single-session self-compassion is inert), Karabinski (higher dose better). No one-off exercises.
- *Recovery-paradox rule.* When the user reports high demand ("no time"/"no energy" most days), do not add recovery actions; reduce what Adler asks for. Recovery goals get added only in weeks with adherence ≥ 80%, or when the user asks.
- *Recovery-module rule.* If a recovery module is ever built, copy Ebert 2015's target — perseverative cognition and sleep effort (worry-time, a wind-down boundary, cutting work-related activity in non-work time) — not generic relaxation audio. That is the only unguided digital recovery programme with a large controlled effect.
- *Deload framing.* AH's deload is also a recovery experience; frame it as **control** ("you choose the week"), since control predicts vigour best (Bennett 2018).
- *Mindfulness rule.* If a user adds meditation, treat it as any other action; at the first weekly review ask "anything unpleasant come up?" (Britton: 58% negative-valence experiences, 6–14% lasting); never claim it improves focus or output.

**What to avoid.**
- "Don't feel that way", "push through the feeling", "just be disciplined" (suppressing the experience d = −0.04; SR).
- Arrival language: "once you hit X you'll feel Y", "imagine how good it'll feel" (impact bias; positive-fantasy backfire, CF).
- Gratitude journals, quote cards, daily affirmations, positivity ratios (g = 0.19 at best; the ratio is a withdrawn model; and they read as cheerleading — D9).
- Diagnosing burnout, ADHD or "productivity dysmorphia"; using MBI language with users.
- Selling detachment as a productivity hack (it slightly costs creativity and discretionary effort), or rest as one more task.
- Building a "regulation fit" score. The person-level version of that idea failed a well-powered preregistered replication.
- Shipping an SCS-style self-compassion score. Half its items measure self-criticism and correlate with distress by construction.

**Adapting to one person over time.**
- Store, per action: during-affect trend, barrier mix, and which dose change moved affect. Affect that improves when dose drops = a good goal at the wrong intensity. Affect that stays flat across every dose = a borrowed goal (GD).
- Store, per user: which regulator the coach used before a return within 7 days (label, perspective-take, distract, change the setting), and stop using the ones that never precede a return. Heiy & Cheavens: the most-used strategy is rarely the most effective *for that person* — and since the a-priori fit model does not replicate, per-user empiricism is the only defensible personalisation.
- Store post-milestone affect: if satisfaction lasts under a week twice, move the goal's unit of review from outcome to process (Klug & Maier: progress > attainment) and re-ask the "what was this for" question (Niemiec).
- Keep the monthly trio as a slow trend line; a two-point drop on the Dolan item is a change signal even inside the normal range.
- Emotion words the user actually uses are a free granularity measure. A user whose vocabulary collapses to "bad/fine" over weeks is a candidate for the one-word prompt and for closer WHO-5 monitoring.

## Conflicts and open questions

1. **Carr 2021 vs White 2019 vs Choi 2025 on PPIs.** g = 0.39 (347 studies) vs r ≈ .10 after small-sample correction vs g = 0.19 for the most-copied PPI in a preregistered 28-country meta. Favour the smaller estimates for non-clinical self-help; Carr's larger effects come from clinical samples with human delivery.
2. **Maslach vs Bianchi on burnout and depression.** Distinct constructs vs r = .80 on exhaustion. Favour Bianchi for the exhaustion core; keep Maslach's cynicism and inefficacy as coaching-relevant descriptors. Consequence: Adler screens, it does not treat.
3. **Strategy-situation fit.** Troy 2013 and Haines 2016 (both small) vs Willroth 2025 (N = 285, preregistered, null). Favour the replication. Open: whether fit matters *within* person over time even if it does not distinguish people — nobody has tested it that way, and Adler's logs could.
4. **Adaptation universal (Brickman) vs event-specific (Luhmann, Lindqvist).** Favour the large data: some gains persist, some decay fast, negative events adapt slowest. "You'll adapt to anything" is not a coaching truth.
5. **Affect during vs after.** Rhodes & Kates say during; "I always feel better afterwards" (Liao) is true but does not predict repetition. Favour during; measure it.
6. **Detachment as pure good.** Wendsche's small negative correlations with creativity and contextual performance, and Fritz 2010's curvilinearity, argue for medium detachment. Untested outside employee samples — every study is occupational.
7. **Self-compassion vs active controls.** Kirby holds, Wilson does not; Phillips & Hine say single sessions are inert; and the SCS total-score debate (Muris & Otgaar vs Neff 2019) means some of the effect is self-criticism measured twice. Open whether a coach's sustained stance counts as a "multi-session intervention"; no trial has tested it.
8. **Self-affirmation.** Meta-analyses positive, best replication null. Open whether a one-line value reminder before hard feedback does anything in a coaching app; cheap to A/B.
9. **Mindfulness apps.** Effects sit at the digital-placebo boundary, follow-up effects are non-significant, and adverse events are unmeasured in app trials while being common (83%) in supervised programmes when measured properly.
10. **Does coaching itself reduce exhaustion?** Collett 2026 says yes for physicians (SMD −0.37, low certainty) — but that is human coaching, in a clinical workforce, >4 weeks. Whether an AI coach inherits any of that is unknown, and it is the most valuable open question in this document for Adler's positioning.
11. **All recovery evidence is occupational.** Whether goal pursuit itself needs "detachment from goals" is untested; the analogy to work-related activity in non-work time (r = −.31 with detachment) is plausible, not shown.
12. **"Productivity dysmorphia" and productivity anxiety.** Still no construct, no prevalence, no validated measure as of 2026. Nearest proxies: the Dolan item, contingent self-worth, and the HAP appreciation question. Open whether the audience's felt problem is burnout's inefficacy dimension or contingent self-worth; Adler's monthly trio can tell them apart over months.
