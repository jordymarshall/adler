## 1. The size of the effect after bias correction

**Mertens, Herberz, Hahnel & Brosch 2022** (*PNAS* 119(1):e2107346118, as corrected at 119(19):e2204059119; k = 447 effect sizes, n = 2,148,439): nudging overall **d = 0.43 [0.38, 0.48]**; defaults 0.62, reminders 0.29, commitment 0.23. The same paper reports Egger's **b = 2.10 [1.31, 2.89]** and its own sensitivity analysis puts the truth at **d = 0.31 under moderate publication bias and d = 0.08 under severe**. **A** — and the correction matters: the original included four observations from a since-retracted paper.

**Maier, Bartoš, Stanley, Shanks, Harris & Wagenmakers 2022** (*PNAS* 119(31):e2200300119) re-ran that dataset through robust Bayesian meta-analysis: **d = 0.04 [0.00, 0.14]** (BF₀₁ = 0.95), or 0.11 [0.00, 0.24] using one estimate per study. "Decision information" fell to **0.00 [0.00, 0.00], BF₀₁ = 33.84** — evidence *for* the null; "decision assistance" likewise; only "decision structure" stayed undecided (0.12 [0.00, 0.43]). Strong evidence of publication bias in every subdomain but food. **Szaszi, Higney, Charlton, Gelman, Ziano, Aczel, Goldstein, Yeager & Tipton 2022** (*PNAS* 119(31):e2200732119) got d = −0.01 (Andrews–Kasy), 0.07 (WAAP) and 0.08 (trim-and-fill), and made the sharper point: under severe bias, 95% of study effects lie **±1.00 around a mean of 0.08**. The variance is the finding. **Mertens et al.'s reply** (119(31):e2202928119) conceded bias and heterogeneity but did not withdraw 0.43. The critics have the better of it — the original authors' own sensitivity analysis already reached 0.08 at the bias level their own Egger test indicates.

**DellaVigna & Linos 2022** (*Econometrica* 90(1):81–116; **126 RCTs, >23M people**, two US government nudge units) settles it from the field: nudge-unit trials moved behaviour **1.4 percentage points on a 17.4 pp base (8.1% relative)** against **8.7 pp (33.5%)** in the academic-journal comparison sample — a **~6× gap**, attributed to publication bias and low power. Practitioners forecast field results accurately; academics did not. **A/B**, and the most useful number here. Framing: **Bryan, Tipton & Yeager 2021** (*Nat Hum Behav* 5:980–989, **D**) — heterogeneity is the rule, so averages without a moderation strategy mislead; **Hallsworth 2023** (*Nat Hum Behav* 7:310–322, **D**) is the practitioner's version.

**Calibrated sentence for the method:** *For an individual-level, digital, self-selected behaviour-change product, the defensible expectation is d ≈ 0.1–0.2 — a percentage point or two on a base rate — while contact continues, mostly gone once it stops, and larger for users who already have slack than for those who do not.*

## 2. The i-frame / s-frame argument, and its best rebuttals

**Chater & Loewenstein 2023** (*BBS* 46:e147, **D**) recant their own field: individual-level ("i-frame") results are "disappointingly modest," and worse, i-framing *deflects* support from system-level ("s-frame") change — "a long-established objective of corporate opponents" of regulation. The empirical anchor for deflection is **Hagmann, Ho & Loewenstein 2019** (*Nature Climate Change* 9:484–489, **B**): a green-energy default reduced support for a carbon tax.

The ~30 peer commentaries hold the strongest counter-arguments. **Hallsworth** (e158): the distinction is "incoherent"; practitioners already do systemic work. **Thaler** (e177): s-frame change needs laws and politics, and the retirement-savings case the article uses *is* systemic. **Kristal & Davidai** (e166) give the most useful cut — not individual vs systemic but **behaviour-changing vs hearts-and-minds**; education-based i-frame work is the weak kind. **Hagger & Hamilton** (e157), **Collier et al.** (e154) and **Zhao & Chen** (e180): the frames are complements, s-frame effects plateau, and i-frame work can *build* support for reform. **Roberto** (e173) and **Strohminger & Táíwò** (e175) press the argument further. The authors' response (e181) narrows to a defensible core: behavioural science's best contribution is building support for systemic reform. Crowding-out itself is thin — a four-wave Australian panel (Ghasemi et al., preprint, N = 2,778) finds individual climate action *positively* associated with policy support. **D**, unreplicated either way. **Marteau, Ogilvie, Roland, Suhrcke & Kelly 2011** (*BMJ* 342:d228) and **Marteau, Rutter & Marmot 2021** (*BMJ* 372:n332) hold the position that fits Adler: individual change is a necessary component, not a substitute for changing conditions, and must be evaluated for its distributional effect.

**The defensible position for a product that is unavoidably i-frame.** Adler is a paid, individual, hands-and-habits product and cannot be otherwise. Three duties follow: (a) do not claim to solve problems whose binding constraint is structural; (b) when a user's binding constraint *is* structural, name it and stop applying dose/cue/plan machinery; (c) be honest about who it works for. Kristal & Davidai's cut is the escape hatch — Adler's core moves (if-then plans, environment change, recorded progress) are the behaviour-changing kind, the sub-class of i-frame work that best survives the critique.

## 3. Does an intervention like Adler widen gaps?

Yes, on current evidence, and this is the most product-shaping section here.

**Lorenc, Petticrew, Welch & Tugwell 2013** (*JECH* 67:190–193, **A**, overview of systematic reviews) named **intervention-generated inequalities**: media campaigns and workplace smoking bans show evidence of *widening* SES gaps; structural workplace change, resource provision and fiscal measures narrow them. Downstream widens, upstream narrows. **Adams, Mytton, White & Monsivais 2016** (*PLoS Medicine* 13(4):e1001990, **D**) give the mechanism — an **agency continuum**: interventions needing low individual agency to benefit (price, reformulation) are more effective *and* more equitable; high-agency ones (education, counselling, self-tracking) accrue to those with time, money, literacy and slack. Adler sits at the extreme high-agency end. **McGill et al. 2015** (*BMC Public Health* 15:457, **A**; 36 studies reporting by socioeconomic position) tested this on diet: "Price" interventions worked best in lower-SEP groups; "Person" interventions had greater impact **with increasing SEP**, and all four dietary-counselling studies were judged likely to **widen** inequalities.

**Western, Armstrong, Islam, Morgan, Jones & Kelson 2021** (*IJBNPA* 18:148, **A**; 19 RCTs) is the closest analogue to Adler: digital physical-activity interventions produced **SMD 0.34 [0.22, 0.45] in high-SES and 0.06 [−0.08, 0.20] in low-SES participants**, heterogeneity near zero, and **neither the number nor the type of BCTs moderated it** — better technique does not fix this. **Zainal, Wang, Garthwaite & Curtiss 2026** (*Health Psychology Review* 20:151–171, **A**; 117 trials, 1,698 effect sizes, 279,791 participants) supplies the engagement gradient: **uptake** ↔ higher socioeconomic status, motivation, lower severity; **completion** ↔ older age, **higher education**, employment, lower stress. Differential attrition by education is the field's central tendency, not a quirk of one trial (cf. AI §5: median 30-day retention 3.3%; survivors are selected). **Frohlich & Potvin 2008** (*AJPH* 98:216–221, **D**) is the theory — a universally available good tool is converted into benefit at different rates across the distribution, so it widens the gap it meant to close.

Two consequences for a ~$10/month subscription aimed at optimizers. Adler's *market* is people with slack, which is where the effect is real — commercially convenient, epistemically fine, and not to be laundered into a claim that the method works for everyone. And the gradient runs *inside* the user base: sold to a shift worker and to a salaried remote worker, the identical product produces different outcomes, and the rule engine reads the difference as effort unless told otherwise.

## 4. Scarcity, bandwidth and time: what survived

**Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir & Zhao 2013** (*Science* 341:976–980, **B**): financial-concern priming reduced cognitive performance among poor but not well-off shoppers; Indian sugarcane farmers performed worse pre- than post-harvest. **O'Donnell et al. 2021** (*PNAS* 118(44):e2103313118 — journal, 23-author list and article number verified as the brief states) ran a preregistered **empirical audit**: 20 replications of studies citing Mani, each at **2.5× the original N**. **Four of 20 reached significance**; effects were smaller in 80% and directionally opposite in 30%; only 9 of 20 originals had 33% power inside the 95% CI. The four successes shared a signature — **economic scarcity manipulations paired with financial decisions**. They did **not** attempt the field studies, so this indicts online scarcity *priming*, not Mani's farmer panel. **Carvalho, Meier & Wang 2016** (*AER* 106(2):260–284, **B**) is the cleaner direct test: low-income US households randomised to survey before or after payday showed **no differences in cognitive function, decision quality, risk-taking or heuristic judgment**; more present bias for money, **none for real-effort tasks** — and effort is Adler's domain.

**Net:** "poverty eats bandwidth, so be gentle" is not a claim Adler may make. What survives is duller and more useful — money constrains *money* decisions. Constrained users do not have less capacity; they have **fewer degrees of freedom**: a COM-B *opportunity* deficit (CF §2), not a capability one, so the response is structural, not therapeutic.

**Time.** MVP §6 already rejects the buying-time result and grades **Giurge, Whillans & West 2020** (*Nat Hum Behav* 4:993–1003) as a perspective piece; nothing here reinstates it. What is primary and hard: **BLS American Time Use Survey 2025** (released 25 June 2026, **C**) — 87% of women and 75% of men did household activities on an average day (2.8 vs 2.1 hours on the days they did), and among adults with a child under six women gave **2.8 hours** of primary childcare a day to men's **1.7**. And **Schneider & Harknett 2019** (*ASR* 84(1):82–114) with **2020** (*Social Forces* 99(4):1682–1709, N = 37,263 hourly service-sector workers, **C**): routine schedule unpredictability predicts distress, poor sleep and unhappiness **more strongly than low wages do**, plus hunger and housing, medical and utility hardship. For a real share of a 16–40 audience — parents of small children, shift and gig workers, carers — "no time" is a measured fact about the week, not a motivational report.

## 5. What adjacent professions already require

**NBHWC Content Outline 2026–2030** (**D**) is the strongest anchor, and Adler's protocol currently satisfies none of it. Verbatim: **3.8.3** "Assist client to identify and develop structural and environmental supports"; **3.8.4 "Recognize non-modifiable systemic barriers to inform supports"**; **3.9.9** "Adapt goals or action plans to address the impact of social determinants of health." Domain 5.4–5.7 requires knowledge of structural determinants ("root causes") and SDOH ("shaped by a set of forces beyond the control of the individual"). The Scope of Practice sets the counterweight — the coach is "an accountability partner, not director," and does not "consult and advise, or provide psychological therapeutic interventions." **3.8.4 is the professional-standards basis for the constraint check below.**

**ICF Core Competencies 2025** (**D**): **4.01** "Seeks to understand the client within their context which may include their identity, environment, experiences, values and beliefs"; competency 6 is defined as listening "in the context of the client systems"; **8.05** "…how to move forward, including resources, support and potential barriers"; **3.12** revisit the agreement when needed; **1.06/1.07** boundaries and referral. Context is a listening obligation, and renegotiating or ending the engagement is in scope.

**Motivational interviewing.** Miller & Rollnick 2009 (*Behav Cogn Psychother* 37:129–140, **D**) list among the ten things MI is not: "a way of tricking people into doing what you want them to do" and "a panacea." MI resolves *ambivalence*; where there is none — the person wants to and cannot — evoking change talk is MI-inconsistent and, per Magill's process meta-analysis (CF §3), raises sustain talk, which predicts worse outcomes. Reflect the constraint, do not argue with it.

**Behavioural activation** is the tradition built for restricted environments — depression as reduced response-contingent reinforcement, and the core skill is telling *avoidance* (approach it) from *genuine restriction* (change the environment or the goal). Cheap and robust: **Ekers et al. 2014** (*PLoS ONE* 9:e100100, **A**; 26 RCTs, N = 1,524) SMD **−0.74 [−0.91, −0.56]** vs controls, no publication bias detected; **Richards et al. 2016** (COBRA, *Lancet* 388:871–880, **A**; n = 440) BA by junior mental-health workers non-inferior to CBT by trained therapists. Borrow the stance, not the protocol.

## Implications for Adler

### CR-1. The constraint check (new rule, highest precedence in Phase 6)

**Trigger** (either): (a) over 14 days, ≥ 60% of recorded misses are **"no time"** or **"something came up"**, minimum four misses, spanning two consecutive weekly reviews; or (b) the user names a **fixed external demand** at intake or in Talk — a shift pattern, caregiving, a second job, a commute, illness in the household, housing or money that rules the goal out.

**Precedence.** CR-1 fires *before* shrink, re-cue, revisit and re-engage, and suppresses all four for that action for 28 days. An action may be shrunk at most **twice**; a third shrink routes to CR-1. **There is no fourth shrink.**

**Exact wording** (trigger a):
> "Two weeks running, the thing in the way has been time, not the plan. I've already made this smaller once, and making it smaller again doesn't buy you an hour."
> "Three things I can do instead. Change the goal so it fits the week you actually have. Change something around it so it needs less setting up. Or park it with a date and pick it up then."
> "Which of those — or something else?"

**Exact wording** (trigger b — name the constraint, do not moralise):
> "You've told me [the night shifts / your mum's appointments / the second job] is fixed for now. That's not a discipline problem and I'm not going to treat it like one."
> …then the same three moves.

**Park wording** (no guilt, no re-engagement afterwards):
> "Parked, not failed. I'll bring it back on [date] and ask once. Nothing between now and then."

**Never, in a CR-1 turn:** ask WOOP's internal-obstacle question — "What inside you usually gets in the way? Not your schedule, you" (CF Phase 2). That is the i-frame move in its purest form; **suppress it for 28 days** after CR-1 fires. Never offer a smaller version. Never ask how badly they want it.

### CR-2. Hard stops on repeat cycles

- **Re-cue ceiling: 2 per action per 8 weeks.** On a third "forgot"-driven cycle, stop changing the cue: *"We've moved when and where this happens twice and it hasn't stuck. That's usually the action being wrong for your week, not your memory."* Offer weekly rather than daily, folding into a routine that already runs unprompted, or parking. Basis: habituation and message fatigue (AI §1, §4); the Drink Less MRT bought next-hour opens and did not change time to disengagement (LRR §7).
- **Re-engagement ceiling: 3 lapses per goal.** After the third silent lapse on the same goal, one close-out instead of a fourth re-engagement: *"I'll stop nudging on this one. It's here if you want it back — say the word."* Basis: false-hope cycling (LRR §3); repeated failed restarts are the mechanism, not the cure.
- **Shrink floor.** Below the 2-minute floor, shrinking is not a move. Route to CR-1.

### Copy ban list: effort-attribution language

Banned in coach output and in `landing/`: "no excuses" · "you just have to want it" · "everyone has the same 24 hours" · "make time" · "find a way" · "if it mattered enough" · "discipline" · "commitment issue" · "consistency is a choice" · "how badly do you want it" · "what's *really* stopping you" · "you're capable of more" · "the only thing in your way is you" · "unlock your potential" · "transform". Banned as a *move*, not only a phrase: reframing a named external demand as a limiting belief, and running a mindset intervention on a scheduling fact. Extends CF §5's language list and Don't #4.

### Claims ceiling for the method and `landing/`

One sentence, verbatim in the method and compressed on the page:

> **Coaching apps of this kind move behaviour by about a percentage point or two on a base rate while you are using them — d ≈ 0.1–0.2 — mostly disappearing after contact stops, and reliably larger for people who already have slack in their week than for people who do not** (DellaVigna & Linos 2022; Maier et al. 2022; Western et al. 2021).

Landing changes: (1) add a **"What it can't do"** block to §6 of `index.astro` — *"Adler can't give you hours, money, or a different job. If what's in the way is your week rather than your plan, it will say so and stop trying to shrink things."* (2) Soften line 28, "the single best-evidenced move in the goal literature" → **"the most consistently replicated move"** (see Conflicts 2). (3) No outcome claims, no transformation language, no before/after. (4) Any published average must be reported with its split by discretionary time.

### Who Adler is not the right tool for (state plainly in the method)

1. Anyone whose binding constraint is **money, housing, immigration status, or hours they do not control**. A subscription is a cost, and the benefit accrues to people with slack.
2. Anyone with **under roughly 30 minutes a day of uncommitted time** in a normal week. Say it at intake, point at cancellation, do not sell harder.
3. Anyone wanting **therapy, diagnosis or treatment**, or in crisis (CF §5, unchanged).
4. Anyone wanting an **accountability partner who pushes harder after a miss**. Adler goes the other way (D9) and should say so rather than fail the expectation quietly.

### How this helps the coach adapt to one person

One per-user parameter, `constraint_class ∈ {none, episodic, fixed}`, inferred from the 28-day barrier mix plus anything named in Talk and shown back in plain words for correction (D3's playbook). `fixed` permanently reorders the move set: environment before dose; weekly rather than daily cadence by default; parked goals get a dated review, never a re-engagement message; grow thresholds raised. Every CR-1 firing is logged as a hypothesis and checked over 14 days — if adherence recovers after a goal change but not after a shrink, that is a playbook entry the shrink rule alone would never find.

**One new intake question** (Phase 1, after "is anything going on that makes this a hard time"): *"On a normal week, how much of your time is actually yours to spend?"* One free-text line, not scored, never shown back as a number. It seeds `constraint_class` and licenses CR-1 to name the constraint later in the user's own words.

**Measure the gradient.** Report adherence, GAS change and 12-week retention **split** by `constraint_class` and by whether CR-1 ever fired. Publishing only the average is how an intervention-generated inequality stays invisible (Lorenc 2013; McGill 2015).

## Conflicts and open questions

1. **Mertens vs Maier is not fully resolved.** Maier's own caveat: heterogeneity survives correction almost everywhere, "which implies that some nudges might be effective, even when there is evidence against the mean effect." Adler's core techniques are not choice-architecture nudges and are not in that dataset; the correction is a prior about the field's reporting, not an estimate of Adler's effect.
2. **Adler's own rank-1 evidence has not had the same treatment.** Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 (d = 0.65, CF §1) predates modern bias correction and, as far as this search found, has never been re-analysed with selection models; Wang et al. 2021 (MCII, g = 0.34) warns its own effect is inflated. The repo applied this correction to growth mindset, ego depletion and self-distancing but not to its own top technique. **Open task before the method quotes 0.65.**
3. **Is CR-1 net-helpful or a licence to quit?** Barlow, Wrosch & McGrath 2020 (SRT §1) put disengagement at r = 0.08 with quality of life, mostly via fewer negative states, and re-engagement at 0.19. Letting go helps little unless something is picked up, so CR-1's three moves must always end in a *next* thing and "park with a date" must actually return. Untested.
4. **The thresholds are judgement, not evidence.** 60% / two weeks / two re-cues / three lapses have no trial behind them; treat them as pre-registered guesses revised against the intervention log.
5. **Self-report is the only constraint signal.** "No time" that really means "didn't feel like it" gets a goal change instead of a nudge (CF §2 already flags the ambiguity). CR-1 deliberately accepts that false positive: mislabelling a real constraint as a character flaw is the worse error.
6. **Crowding-out is weakly evidenced both ways** (Hagmann 2019 is one study; the counter is a preprint) — cite neither. And the direct evidence that effort-attribution language *harms* is indirect: **Godfrey, Santos & Burson 2019** (*Child Development* 90:180–195; n = 257 low-income adolescents) found system-justifying beliefs predicted *worse* two-year trajectories of self-esteem and behaviour, but the sample and age are far from Adler's users. **C** for transfer.
7. **Equity vs the business model.** The evidence says the product works best for people who need it least. Nothing here resolves that; it only forbids concealing it.
