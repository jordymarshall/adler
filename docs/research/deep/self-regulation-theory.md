# Self-regulation theory: control loops, self-efficacy, and self-control strategies

Research date: 2026-09-01. Companion to `coaching-framework.md` (CF §n), `atomic-habits-and-training-science.md` (AH), `deep/humanistic-existential-common-factors.md` (HE) and `goal-discovery-frameworks.md` (GD). CF §1 ranks implementation intentions, monitoring, goal setting, WOOP, SDT, habit formation and situational strategies; CF §7 and AH Part C set the shrink/grow thresholds. This file covers the machinery underneath: how progress is compared with a standard, why rate rather than distance drives mood, where confidence comes from and when it misleads, what survived the ego-depletion collapse, and how self-control strategies should be classified and fitted. Effect sizes are d/g unless stated. Tags: **MA** meta-analysis, **RRR** registered multi-lab replication, **FE** randomised/field experiment, **ESM** experience sampling, **C** correlational, **T** theory.

## 1. Control theory (Carver & Scheier)

**Core model (T).** Behaviour is a negative feedback loop: reference value, perceived input, comparator, output that reduces the discrepancy (Carver & Scheier 1981, 1998; the TOTE unit of Miller, Galanter & Pribram 1960). Goals form a hierarchy: system concepts (who I am) → principles (be healthy) → programs (run three times a week) → sequences (shoes on). Adler's stack (identity → goal → action → cue; GD §11, CF §7) is this hierarchy.

**Second loop: rate, not distance, drives affect (T + FE).** Carver & Scheier 1990 (*Psychological Review*) added a meta-loop that compares the *rate* of discrepancy reduction with an expected rate: faster → positive affect, slower → negative affect; affect is the rate loop's error signal and reallocates effort. Corollary, "coasting": doing better than expected yields positive affect and *less* effort on that goal, freeing attention for others (Carver 2003). Evidence: Hsee & Abelson 1991 (scenarios; satisfaction tracked velocity); Lawrence, Carver & Scheier 2002 (false-feedback experiment; the same end point on a rising trajectory produced more positive affect than on a falling one); Chang, Johnson & Lord 2010 (velocity predicted satisfaction controlling for progress); Johnson, Howe & Chang 2013 (review). Small lab experiments, consistent direction, no MA, untested in a habit product.

**Discrepancy-reducing vs discrepancy-enlarging loops (T + C).** Approach goals close distance to a desired state; avoidance goals *enlarge* distance from a feared state and never end (Carver & Scheier 1998; Carver 2006). Elliot, Sheldon & Church 1997 (two semester-long studies): a higher share of avoidance personal goals predicted lower and declining wellbeing; neuroticism and low perceived life skills predicted adopting them. Elliot & Church 1997: performance-avoidance goals → worse grades and wellbeing. Roskes, Elliot & De Dreu 2014 (review): avoidance demands vigilance, narrows attention, tires. A 2024 MA: performance-avoidance goals ↔ anxiety r = .25, depression r = .16. Chang, Webb, Benn & Stride 2017 (N = 40 / 492 / 481): prevention-focused goals were monitored *less* (B = −0.96) and avoidance goals rated harder to monitor (B = −0.13); quantifiability was the strongest predictor of monitoring; self-efficacy made monitoring feel easier (B = −0.16 to −0.31 on difficulty). A "stop X" goal has no end point and no monitoring.

**Expectancy and disengagement (T + MA).** When the rate loop signals trouble, expectancy is re-evaluated: enough confidence → renewed effort, too little → disengagement (Carver & Scheier 1998), adaptive only when an alternative is taken up. Wrosch, Scheier, Miller, Schulz & Carver 2003: Goal Adjustment Scale (disengagement, reengagement; 10 items). Barlow, Wrosch & McGrath 2020 (MA, 31 samples, 421 effects): disengagement ↔ quality of life r = .08 (mainly fewer negative states, r = −.12); reengagement r = .19 (positive .24, negative −.17); the two capacities correlate only r = .17. Letting go and picking up the next thing are different skills.

**Monitoring, standards, feedback.** CF §1 rank 2 gives Harkin et al. 2016 (MA, 138 RCTs, N = 19,951: attainment d = 0.40 [0.32, 0.48]; monitoring frequency d = 1.98; larger when physically recorded and reported). Extensions:
- *Ostrich problem.* Webb, Chang & Benn 2013 (review); Webb et al. 2017 (survey): people avoid progress information when it would demand change, when progress is poor, and when the goal carries negative emotion. Lapsed users avoid the comparator; they do not forget.
- *Feedback can harm.* Kluger & DeNisi 1996 (MA, 607 effects, N = 23,663): mean d = 0.41, but over one-third of feedback interventions *reduced* performance. Feedback that moves attention to the self (praise, person-level evaluation, normative comparison) harms; feedback on task and process helps. This is the mechanism behind CF Don't 2.
- *Framing progress.* Koo & Fishbach 2008 (JPSP): when commitment is uncertain, "to-date" framing (what is done) motivates; when commitment is secure, "to-go" framing (what remains) motivates. Koo & Fishbach 2012 (JCR, small-area hypothesis): attend to whichever is smaller, accomplished early and remaining near the end. Finkelstein & Fishbach 2012 (JCR): novices seek positive feedback (builds commitment), experts negative feedback (signals the gap). Several small experiments from one lab, not preregistered.

## 2. Self-efficacy (Bandura)

**Construct (T).** Bandura 1977, 1997: belief in one's capability to execute a specific behaviour in a specific context. Four sources, in claimed order: enactive mastery, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, physiological/affective states. Task efficacy (can I do it) and barrier efficacy (can I do it tired, travelling, stressed) are distinct.

**Between-person association (MA, C).** Stajkovic & Luthans 1998 (114 studies, N = 21,616): work performance r = .38 (their converted d ≈ 0.82). Moritz et al. 2000 (45 studies): sport r = .38. Multon, Brown & Lent 1991: academic r ≈ .38. All confounded with ability and past performance.

**Changing efficacy changes behaviour (MA of RCTs).** Sheeran et al. 2016: experimentally raised self-efficacy changed intentions d = 0.51 and behaviour d = 0.47. Ashford, Edmunds & French 2010 (27 studies, N = 5,501): physical-activity interventions raised efficacy only d = 0.16; feedback on past or others' performance and vicarious experience went with *higher* efficacy; *persuasion, graded mastery and barrier identification* with lower. Williams & French 2011 (27 studies): d = 0.16 on efficacy, 0.21 on activity; action planning, instruction and reinforcing effort raised both; relapse prevention and graded tasks lowered both; technique effects on efficacy and behaviour correlated r_s = .69. Higgins et al. 2014 (20 RCTs): task efficacy g = 0.21, barrier efficacy g = 0.13, activity g = 0.34; task efficacy drove adoption, barrier efficacy maintenance; interventions that did *not* require daily exercise logs did better. Coaching raises self-efficacy g = 0.59 (Wang et al. 2022; HE §7).

**Sources, quantified (MA, C).** Byars-Winston et al. 2017 (model-based MA, k = 61, N = 8,965): performance accomplishments show "by far the strongest unique association". Sheu et al. 2018 (104 studies, 141 samples): direct experience (mastery, persuasion, affect) vs vicarious; vicarious has a weak or negative unique effect. Egele, Klopp & Stark 2025 (N = 335, dominance analysis, physical activity): mastery 18.4% of explained variance, verbal *self*-persuasion 15.8%, positive affect 11.8%, negative affect 10.7%, vicarious 2.3%, persuasion *by others* 1.0%. Arranged wins and feedback on one's own record dominate; other people saying you can is close to worthless.

**The within-person problem (C, FE, MA).** Vancouver, Thompson & Williams 2001; Vancouver & Kendall 2006: *within* a person, higher efficacy on one occasion predicted lower effort and performance on the next (complacency via reduced resource allocation). Bandura & Locke 2003 and Bandura 2012 called this artefact. Sitzmann & Yeo 2013 (MA of within-person studies): efficacy → performance is small and shrinks toward zero once past performance is controlled; past performance → efficacy is the stronger path (coefficients not verified from the abstract). Talsma et al. 2018 (cross-lagged MA, k = 11, N = 2,688): performance → later efficacy β = .205; efficacy → later performance β = .071; reciprocal in adults only. Schmidt & DeShon 2010: the negative within-person effect appears under performance ambiguity and reverses with unambiguous feedback. Beck & Schmidt 2018 (two studies, one experimental): pulling back effort when confident is adaptive when time is scarce. Net: self-efficacy is mostly a *readout* of recent mastery and a good between-person predictor; within a person, rising confidence with falling effort is a real failure mode, most likely when feedback is vague.

## 3. Ego depletion: what collapsed and what remains

**Rise and fall.** Strength model (Baumeister et al. 1998; Muraven & Baumeister 2000): self-control draws on a limited resource; Hagger et al. 2010 MA d = 0.62 across 198 tests. Carter & McCullough 2014: after PET-PEESE bias correction, indistinguishable from zero. Hagger et al. 2016 (**RRR**, 23 labs, N = 2,141): d = 0.04 [−0.07, 0.15]. Vohs et al. 2021 (**RRR**, 36 labs, N = 3,531, paradigms chosen by proponents): confirmatory d = 0.06 [−0.02, 0.14], Bayes factor 4:1 for the null; full sample d = 0.08; one sub-measure d = 0.15; manipulations produced strong felt effort (d = 0.46 and 3.09) but no downstream deficit; no moderation by willpower beliefs, trait self-control or action orientation; larger effects in participants reporting more fatigue. Job, Dweck & Walton 2010 (depletion only in people who believe willpower is limited) failed a preregistered replication (PLOS One 2023) and did not moderate in Vohs 2021. Friese, Loschelder, Gieseler, Frankenbach & Inzlicht 2019 (PSPR): "the available evidence is inconclusive."

**Rebuttal.** Baumeister, André, Southwick & Tice 2024 (*Curr Opin Psychol*): replicability "has now been well established" with "longer, stronger manipulations to ensure fatigue"; the theory is reframed as conservation, not exhaustion. Their best evidence is Dang et al. 2025 (14 samples, N = 2,078; 30–40 min antisaccade then Go/No-Go): d = 0.31–0.35, I² = 0, BF₁₀ > 700. Lin, Saunders, Friese, Evans & Inzlicht 2020 (four preregistered within-subject studies, N = 686): a strong effort manipulation reduced *response caution* (faster, sloppier), not processing speed.

**Where the evidence sits.** The classic two-short-tasks effect is zero or trivial; a fatigue-after-long-demand effect of d ≈ 0.3 is plausible and preregistered. Mechanistically it looks like a *motivational shift*, not an emptied tank: Inzlicht & Schmeichel 2012; Inzlicht, Schmeichel & Macrae 2014 (after effort, motivation shifts from have-to toward want-to, attention toward rewards); Kurzban, Duckworth, Kable & Myers 2013 (felt effort is the opportunity cost of forgone alternatives). Direct tests of the process model are few and mixed. Both camps imply the same coaching rule: do not build the plan on willpower; read "no energy" as load and motive, not character.

**Trait self-control works through habits (MA, ESM).** de Ridder et al. 2012 (102 studies, N = 32,648): trait self-control ↔ behaviour r ≈ .30, *stronger for automatic than controlled behaviour*. Hofmann, Baumeister, Förster & Vohs 2012 (ESM, N = 205, 7,827 reports): high self-control people report fewer and weaker conflicting desires, not more successful resistance. Galla & Duckworth 2015 (six studies, N = 2,274): habits mediate the self-control → outcome link. Milyavskaya & Inzlicht 2017 (CF rank 8): effortful resistance did not predict attainment. Job et al. 2015 (longitudinal, C): a "nonlimited" willpower belief predicted better self-regulation under high demands, but its lab basis did not replicate; teach neither belief.

## 4. Self-control strategies: taxonomy and fit

**Process model (T + FE).** Duckworth, Gendler & Gross 2016: an impulse develops through situation → attention → appraisal → response; strategies intervene at each stage (situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change, response modulation); earlier is better because the impulse is weak. Duckworth, White, Matteucci, Shearer & Gross 2016 (JEP): students rated early strategies "dramatically more effective"; in two field experiments, students randomised to situation modification met more weekly academic goals than those told to use willpower or given nothing, d = 0.56–0.63, partly mediated by lower felt temptation; self-report, one week.

**The 2 × 2 (Duckworth, Milkman & Laibson 2018, PSPI).** Strategies are *situational* (change the objective environment) or *cognitive* (change the mental representation), and *self-deployed* or *other-deployed*. Self-deployed situational: commitment devices, temptation bundling, removing or distancing temptations. Self-deployed cognitive: goal setting, implementation intentions, MCII, self-monitoring, psychological distancing, mindfulness, reappraisal. Other-deployed situational: defaults, microenvironments, active choice, choosing in advance, planned interruptions, incentives, hard paternalism. Other-deployed cognitive: reminders, norms, framing, education. Cited effects are modest: smoking deposit contracts +3 percentage points passing a urine test (Giné, Karlan & Zinman 2010); grocery precommitment +3.5 points healthy purchases (Schwartz et al. 2014); implementation-intention prompts d = 0.09 (flu shot) and 0.04 (colonoscopy) (Milkman et al. 2011, 2013); MCII for activity d = 0.47 (Stadler et al. 2010). Their caveats: situational strategies suit *physical* temptations and work best when costly to reverse; cognitive strategies suit *internal* temptations and are easily reversed; self-deployed strategies burden the person but build transferable capacity; head-to-head comparisons are rare.

**Daily-life evidence (ESM).** Hennecke, Czikmantori & Brandstätter 2019 (N = 264, 1,940 reports): high trait self-control people more often focused on positive consequences, set goals and regulated emotion, but strategy use did not explain the trait effect. Wenzel, Bürgler, Brandstätter, Kreibich & Hennecke 2024 (14,067 conflicts, 22 strategies): eight strategies were positively associated with success (e.g., task enrichment and, unexpectedly, suppression), three negatively (e.g., putting oneself under pressure); five strategies' efficacy depended on conflict type (initiation, persistence, inhibition); people who created *strategy-situation fit* reported more success. Milyavskaya, Saunders & Inzlicht 2021 (N = 197): all six studied strategies worked; strategies were combined in 25% of conflicts. Bürgler, Hoyle & Hennecke 2021 (N = 226, 10 days): metacognitive knowledge, repertoire and feedback monitoring each predicted success; Friese, Bürgler, Hofmann & Hennecke 2024 (review): broader repertoire ↔ better outcomes. The refinement to Duckworth: not "situational beats cognitive" but "match strategy to conflict, hold a repertoire, notice whether it worked".

## 6. Implications for Adler

**Show the rate, not the distance.** The daily unit stays binary (CF Phase 4); the weekly review runs the rate loop: "4 of 6 this week, 3 of 6 last week"; for project goals the weekly GAS delta (CF §6) is the velocity. Rule (H on E): one week below the prior rate → one question ("What was different?"), no plan change; two consecutive down weeks → CF Phase 6.

**Calibrate the expected rate so most weeks beat it slightly.** Negative affect follows progress slower than *expected*, whatever the absolute level. The floor dose and the ≥ 7 confidence gate (CF Phase 3, AH Part C) do this; the method should say why: small positive discrepancies sustain effort.

**Use coasting on purpose.** For a one-goal user, "ahead of rate" plus rising ease is a coasting risk: hold the plan, no celebration. For the multi-goal optimiser it is the hand-off signal: ≥ 80% for two weeks, SRBAI ≥ 5/7 and "easier than expected" on 5 of 7 days → offer the next goal (CF Phase 7) and move this one to weekly maintenance. This is how many goals are held without many *builds* (AH concurrent-actions rule).

**Convert every anti-goal into an approach goal with a countable unit.** A stop/quit/less goal gets a paired "do Y instead at cue Z", and Y is what is monitored. Ask: "What would you be doing in that time instead?" Never headline a count of the avoided behaviour.

**Monitoring costs nothing and stays on the task.** D3's one tap fits Harkin (record it) and Higgins 2014 (mandatory logs hurt). From Kluger & DeNisi: name the action and the strategy, never the person; no comparison with other users; no cumulative deficits. Ostrich rule: silence after a miss is avoidance of the comparator; CF Phase 9's script stands, and the re-entry question is about the plan ("same, smaller, or a different time?"), not the gap.

**Frame progress by phase.** Weeks 1–2 (commitment uncertain): to-date framing, affirm what went right, SF "what's better?" (HE §6). After SRBAI ≥ 4/7: to-go framing and plain gap talk ("two days short of the rate you set; what got in the way?"). Dated project goals switch from accomplished to remaining past the halfway point. Never mix both in one message.

**Move the confidence ruler with the user's own record.** The ruler is a mastery readout. Highest-yield move: feedback on past performance ("on a week with two late nights you still did it four times"). Shrink until a win is near-certain (CF Phase 3), then cite the win. Coach persuasion is the weakest source and went with *lower* efficacy in trials: a mechanistic reason for D9. Vicarious examples are optional, small, never invented. Confidence dropping with no adherence change → ask about sleep and energy first. Ask two rulers: "How sure are you for tomorrow?" (task efficacy, adoption) and "How sure are you on a bad day?" (barrier efficacy, maintenance).

**Watch for the complacency signature.** Confidence ≥ 9 with adherence falling, or "easier than expected" most days with misses creeping in, is the within-person pattern (Vancouver; Schmidt & DeShon). It thrives on ambiguous feedback, so answer with unambiguous numbers and one question: "Where is the time going instead?" Never grow the dose on confidence alone.

**Never say "depleted"; teach no willpower belief.** No "willpower is a muscle", no "willpower is unlimited", no "running on empty". Route "no energy" as CF §2 does, plus the process-model reading: reduce the have-to (smaller, earlier), raise the want-to (one line on why; WOOP), or cut concurrent demands (AH weekly load check ≥ 7 → hold all builds). After objectively long demands (10-hour day, exams, newborn) shrink pre-emptively; that is the one condition where an after-effect is credible.

**Ask situational first, then fit the strategy to the conflict.** First question after any barrier: "What could change about the setup so this needs no willpower?" Then classify and offer one option from the matching cell:
- *Initiation* ("didn't feel like it"): object in sight; implementation intention; temptation bundling/task enrichment; 2-minute floor.
- *Persistence* ("too hard", quits midway): proximal sub-goal; time-box with a stated stop; focus on positive consequences; "what's better" after partial days.
- *Inhibition* (distraction, competing habit): remove or distance the cue; opt-in precommitment (CF rank 12); distancing ("what would you tell a friend?").
Never prescribe self-pressure ("no excuses"): it is one of the three strategies negatively associated with success. Internal temptations belong in the cognitive cell; do not present situational strategies as always superior.

**Build the repertoire and log the fit.** Barrier → strategy → next-7-days outcome is already logged (D3). Add conflict type, strategy cell, and self-deployed vs coach-suggested. Over time the coach learns the user's dominant conflict type, which cell moves their adherence, which efficacy source moves their ruler, and whether down weeks predict silence. Confirmed pairs become the playbook; CF Phase 8's "what did you learn about what works for you?" is the flexibility literature's active ingredient.

**Screen goal-adjustment capacity when goals pile up.** At more than three goals, or "didn't feel like it" 3+ times in 14 days (CF Phase 6), ask two Wrosch-derived items: "How easy is it for you to let a goal go?" and "How quickly do you find something new to aim at?" Low disengagers get a *park* ritual ("parked, not failed; review on the 1st"); high reengagers can be handed the next goal fast.

**Thresholds proposed here (H on E):** down-week question after one week below prior rate, Phase 6 after two; coasting hand-off at ≥ 80% × 2 weeks + SRBAI ≥ 5 + "easier" 5/7; complacency check at confidence ≥ 9 with adherence < 60% over 7 days; barrier-efficacy ruler < 6 → build a bad-day version first; pre-emptive shrink after any reported ≥ 10-hour demand day.

## 7. Conflicts and open questions

- **Bandura vs Vancouver.** Bandura: negative within-person effects are artefacts. Vancouver, Sitzmann & Yeo, Talsma: performance → efficacy is the larger path; within-person efficacy → performance is small and sometimes negative under ambiguity. Evidence favours the second view for within-person adaptation and Bandura for between-person prediction; Adler reads the ruler both ways.
- **Ego depletion: Baumeister 2024 vs Hagger/Vohs.** Proponents claim replication with long manipulations (Dang 2025, d ≈ 0.3); two RRRs at d ≈ 0.05 used paradigms proponents chose. Evidence favours "no general limited resource; a modest fatigue-and-motivation after-effect of long demands". Both imply the same product behaviour.
- **Situational > cognitive?** Duckworth's hierarchy rests on student self-report and one-week field experiments; daily-life ESM finds cognitive strategies adaptive and efficacy conflict-dependent. Evidence favours *fit* over a fixed ranking; head-to-head trials are missing.
- **More monitoring vs mandatory logs.** Harkin: more is better; Higgins 2014: required daily logs did worse. Resolution: near-free, behavioural monitoring (D3). Whether a one-tap check-in itself triggers the ostrich problem in bad weeks is untested.
- **Velocity display vs quitting.** Showing rate may amplify negative affect after a bad week and, via Silverman & Barasch (CF §1), turn a dip into abandonment. Test "4 of 6 vs 3 of 6" against a plain count in the first cohort; measure retention after the first down week.
- **To-date/to-go for open-ended habits.** Koo & Fishbach studied goals with end points; the phase rule is an extrapolation.
- **Persuasion vs affirmation.** Ashford and Egele: persuasion by others adds nothing; MI affirmations (CF §3) have process support. Generic "you can" is persuasion; specific "you did X under Y" is feedback on past performance. The method should define affirmation as the latter.
- **Coasting in a multi-goal product.** Whether inviting effort to migrate from goals that are ahead yields more total attainment or more half-built habits is unknown; the concurrent-build cap (AH Part C) is the hedge.
- **Goal adjustment and retention.** Barlow's correlations are small; whether two Wrosch items predict who churns after a lapse is a cohort question worth answering early.
