# Motivation science for goal pursuit: progress, time, reward timing, and the reasons behind a goal

Research date: 2026-09-01. Companion to `coaching-framework.md` (CF §n), `goal-discovery-frameworks.md` (GDF §n) and `atomic-habits-and-training-science.md` (AH). Those cover implementation intentions, monitoring, autonomy support, self-concordance, habits, streaks and progression; nothing here repeats them. This file covers how motivation *moves* once a concordant goal and a tiny action exist. Effect sizes as reported (r / ρ / d / g). Evidence levels: **A** meta-analysis or several RCTs; **B** field or lab experiments; **C** correlational or theory.

---

## 1. Fishbach, *Get It Done* (2022), audited

The book packages the Chicago goal-systems programme. Verified against the Booth "tiny course" text and two independent summaries.

| Claim | Evidence behind it | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Set a goal, not a means; a goal that names the means "feels like a chore" ("explore career opportunities" beats "read job postings") | Goal-systems theory (Kruglanski 2002); means–end lab studies | C, a framing rule |
| Abstract but not vague; "do" not "don't"; numeric targets that are challenging, measurable, actionable, self-set; beware "malicious" over-ambition | Locke & Latham (CF §1); Elliot (GDF §11); Heath, Larrick & Wu 1999 goals as reference points | A for specificity and approach framing; C for the target-as-loss mechanism |
| The middle problem: motivation sags between start and end; shorten middles (weekly goals), add subgoals | Bonezzi 2011; Touré-Tillery & Fishbach 2012; Huang 2017 (§2) | B, single labs, N = 69–200 |
| All-or-nothing goals (pay-off only at the end) show the U; accumulative goals collect benefit as they go | Same, plus Kivetz 2006 | B/C |
| Look at what is done when commitment is shaky, at what remains when committed; the "ostrich problem" | Koo & Fishbach 2008, 2012; Webb, Chang & Benn 2013 | B |
| Immediate rewards sustain persistence; seek discomfort as a sign of growth | Woolley & Fishbach 2016–2022 (§3) | B |

**"Dry" vs "wet" goals** appears in our brief but in no primary paper, the Booth text, or either summary. Nearest verified constructs: goal-vs-means and delayed-vs-immediate reward. Treat it as our shorthand, not hers. **Verdict:** honest popularisation of a real programme; almost all of it single-lab, N ≈ 70–250, not pre-registered, not independently replicated. Use the framings; never quote its effect sizes in copy.

## 2. Progress dynamics: gradient, middle, framing

**Goal gradient.** Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006 (*JMR*): café loyalty members cut inter-purchase time 0.7 days (20%) from first to last stamp; cards finished in 24.6 days vs 29.4 at the starting rate; 58% were accelerators; rate *reset* after each reward. Field experiment (N = 108): a 12-stamp card with two pre-filled stamps was completed in 12.7 days vs 15.6 for a plain 10-stamp card, same work, 20% faster. Nunes & Drèze 2006 (*JCR*, 300 car-wash cards): 34% vs 19% completion for 2/10 vs 0/8. Kaur, Kremer & Mullainathan 2015 (*JPE*, year-long field experiment): effort rises toward randomly assigned paydays. A 2024 drift-diffusion study: proximity speeds responding without hurting accuracy, but only when proximity is displayed. **B**, replicated across paradigms; all have a fixed, visible end.

**The middle.** Bonezzi, Brendl & De Angelis 2011 (*Psych Sci*, N = 69–200): proofreading with no frame ran 0.122 typos/s near the start, 0.092 halfway, 0.124 near the end (d = 0.55, 0.63); donations under a to-date frame fell from $2.68 at the start to $1.12 in the middle (d = 0.36). A to-go frame gives a rising gradient, a to-date frame a falling one; the U appears when people switch reference points mid-way (diminishing sensitivity). Touré-Tillery & Fishbach 2012 (*JEP:G*): people cut corners in the middle because starts and ends feel diagnostic of who they are. **B**.

**Framing.** Koo & Fishbach 2008 (*JPSP*): to-date information raises adherence when commitment is uncertain; to-go when it is secure. Koo & Fishbach 2012 (*JCR*): attend to whichever portion is smaller (done early, remaining late). Fishbach & Dhar 2005 (*JCR*): reading an action as *progress* licenses indulgence; reading it as *commitment* sustains. Amir & Ariely 2008: progress markers help when distance is uncertain, breed complacency when known. Huang, Jin & Zhang 2017 (*OBHDP*): subgoals help at initiation, a whole-goal focus near completion. Dai 2018 (*OBHDP*, archival sport data plus experiments): resetting a metric to zero raises self-efficacy and performance after a weak period and lowers both after a strong one. All **B**. Addition to CF §1 rank 2 (monitoring d = 0.40): the display frame changes the sign of the same count.

## 3. Immediate rewards, intrinsic motivation, incentives

**Woolley & Fishbach 2017 (*PSPB*, five correlational studies, N ≈ 80–100 each).** People say they pursue resolutions, study and exercise for delayed rewards (70% of library users; d ≈ 1.0 on when the benefit arrives), yet immediate rewards predict persistence and delayed ones do not: resolutions β = .31 vs .14 ns; library hours .43 vs .19; gym minutes .24 vs −.08; weekly cardio .24 vs ns. Delayed rewards predicted *expected* persistence (β = .42 vs .19): what people think will carry them is not what does. Internal meta-analysis: immediate > delayed. **2018 (*JPSP*, experiments N = 120–223)**: an immediate bonus raised intrinsic motivation vs delayed or none (d = 0.56); 84.2% kept reading unpaid after an immediate bonus vs 70.3% after a delayed one (OR 0.44); timing beat size. **2022 (*Psych Sci*, five pre-registered experiments, N = 2,163)**: told to *seek* discomfort as a sign of growth, people persisted longer in improv, expressive writing and reading opposing views. One lab, no outside replication. **B**.

**Cerasoli, Nicklin & Ford 2014 (*Psych Bull*, k = 183, N = 212,468).** Intrinsic motivation → performance ρ = .26 (quality .35, quantity .26); it still predicts when incentives are present (ρ = .30 with directly performance-tied incentives, .45 with indirect ones); incentives buy quantity, intrinsic motivation buys quality. **A**. Not a refutation of Deci 1999 (CF §1): salient, contingent, expected rewards reduce interest in tasks already liked. **Howard et al. 2021 (*Persp Psych Sci*, k = 344, N = 223,209)**: adaptive outcomes ρ = .41 intrinsic, .38 identified, .17 introjected, −.01 external, −.24 amotivation; maladaptive ρ = −.13, −.04, .19, .18, .28 respectively; introjection predicts effort (.25) but not continuation. **A**, correlational.

Reconciliation the evidence favours: rewards hurt when they become *the reason* (salient, contingent, delayed, external) and help when *fused with the activity* (immediate, experiential, self-chosen). "Make it pleasant now" and "don't pay people for what they like" are one rule.

## 4. Want-to versus have-to

**Milyavskaya, Inzlicht, Hope & Koestner 2015 (*JPSP*; IAT N ≈ 137, semester multilevel study, experience sampling 151 students / 3,615 signals).** Want-to motivation (identified + intrinsic) predicted lower automatic attraction to temptations (b = −.12), fewer obstacles (within-person −.22) and progress (+.14), with *no* extra effort (.03). Have-to (external + introjected) predicted more obstacles (+.10) and more effort (+.10), net progress ≈ 0, and *lower* control facing temptation. **Werner et al. 2016**: concordant goals progress via subjective ease, not effort. **Holding et al. 2017 (*J Personality*, N = 425, three goals per semester)**: autonomous motivation → fewer action crises (b = −.22) → progress (b = −.38); controlled motivation → action crises (b = .29) → depressive symptoms (b = .20). **C/B**, prospective, one lab tradition. New relative to CF rank 5/8 and GDF §10: want-to goals do not make people try harder; they make the world less tempting. That is visible in barrier taps.

## 5. Time: temporal motivation theory, discounting, precommitment

**TMT (Steel & König 2006, *AMR*).** Utility = (Expectancy × Value) / (Γ × Delay); expectancy-value with Ainslie's hyperbola in the denominator. **Steel 2007 (*Psych Bull*, 691 correlations)**: procrastination vs conscientiousness r = −.62 (ρ = −.75), self-control −.58, self-handicapping .46, distractibility .45, impulsiveness .41, boredom proneness .40, task aversiveness ≈ .40, self-efficacy −.38, organisation −.36, achievement motivation −.35, intrinsic motivation −.26, neuroticism .24; intention −.03 but intention–action gap .29 and dilatory behaviour .52. Prevalence: 80–95% of students, ~50% problematically; 15–20% of adults chronically. **Steel et al. 2018 (*Frontiers*)**: 171 students over 76 assessment points paced work hyperbolically, steepness predicted by trait procrastination; online N = 7,400: same intentions, larger intention–action gap for distal (R² = .30) than proximal goals (.07); temptation proximity r = .53; emotional distractibility .73, lack of energy .68, attention control −.64, planning −.51; attention, energy and automaticity explained 74% of variance. **Gröpel & Steel 2008 (N = 9,351)**: lack of energy strongest; interest enhancement worked *through* energy; goal setting weaker and partly redundant. **van Eerde & Klingsieck 2018 (24 studies, N = 1,173)**: "large" reduction, stable at follow-up, CBT-type best, duration irrelevant. **A/C**.

**Discounting.** Ainslie 1975 (*Psych Bull*): preference reversals near a reward follow a hyperbola; his later work (1992, 2001) says people beat it by treating each choice as a *precedent* (personal rules, bright lines), which is why "never miss twice" (CF §1) can work as a rule without trial evidence. Green & Myerson 2004: hyperbolas fit human discounting better than exponentials in study after study. Frederick, Loewenstein & O'Donoghue 2002 (*JEL*): implied discount rates vary by orders of magnitude; discounting is not one parameter. **Imai, Rutter & Camerer 2021 (*EJ*, 220 estimates)**: present bias is real but modest, heterogeneous, selectively over-reported, larger for non-monetary rewards. **Augenblick, Niederle & Sprenger 2015 (*QJE*)**: β = 0.974–0.988 for money vs 0.877–0.900 for real effort; effort present-bias predicted demand for binding commitment. **A**. People put off *effort*, not money; that is Adler's domain.

**Precommitment.** Bryan, Karlan & Nelson 2010: a commitment device restricts one's own future choices, hard (money) or soft (psychological). Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002 (N = 99, 60): students self-imposed costly deadlines (only 27% put everything last); self-set deadlines beat none, but imposed evenly spaced deadlines produced the best grades and error detection; people space badly. Kaur 2015: workers chose dominated penalty contracts 36% of the time; output rose as with an 18% pay rise. **Halpern 2015 (*NEJM*, N = 2,538)**: 90.0% accepted rewards, 13.7% deposits; 6-month abstinence: usual care 6.0%, rewards 14.9–16.0%, deposits 9.4–10.9%; among people who would accept either, deposits won by 13.2 points. Duckworth, Milkman & Laibson 2018 (*PSPI*) file commitment devices with situation modification and bundling as *situational, self-deployed* strategies that beat in-the-moment cognitive effort. **A/B**. Commitment works for those who choose it, most do not, and the structural version (regular external deadlines, a fixed slot) beats the heroic one.

## 6. Grit

**Credé, Tynan & Harms 2017 (*JPSP*, 584 effects, 88 samples, N = 66,807)**: grit–academic performance ρ ≈ .18; retention .12–.18; grit–conscientiousness ρ = .84 (up to .97 in Duckworth's samples); higher-order factor not confirmed; perseverance ρ = .26 vs consistency of interest .10; overall grit ΔR² ≈ 0–.004 over conscientiousness, perseverance alone .023–.085. The West Point "99% more likely to complete" was an odds ratio on a 94% base rate. **Lam & Zhou 2022 (*JEP*, 137 studies, N = 285,331)**: r = .19; perseverance .21, consistency .08; no cultural moderation. Duckworth (2016) conceded "small-to-medium" and "a member of the conscientiousness family". **Alan, Boneva & Ertac 2019 (*QJE*, school cluster-RCT)**: a persistence curriculum raised effort and maths ≈ 0.2 SD at 2.5 years. **A**. Grit is conscientiousness re-labelled; perseverance carries what value there is; "stick to one passion" is inert. Telling optimizers with too many goals to be grittier is unsupported and the wrong diagnosis.

## 7. Curiosity and interest

Loewenstein 1994: curiosity as an information gap. Hidi & Renninger 2006: four phases from triggered situational to well-developed individual interest; early phases need outside support. **Tang et al. 2022 (24 studies, N = 4,817)**: curiosity–interest r = .53, very heterogeneous; curiosity clusters with wanting to close a gap, interest with enjoyment and value. **Von Stumm, Hell & Chamorro-Premuzic 2011**: curiosity and effort each predict academic performance at about conscientiousness level (ρ ≈ .20) and together rival intelligence (≈ .35). **Schutte & Malouff 2022 (41 RCTs, N = 4,496)**: curiosity can be raised, g = 0.57 [0.44, 0.70], most by mystery and games; persistence not measured. **Lazowski & Hulleman 2016 (92 effects, N = 38,377)**: education motivation interventions d = 0.49, smaller when randomised; utility-value writing d ≈ 0.24 (2021 meta) and 0.08–0.20 in recent RCTs. Gruber et al. 2014 (*Neuron*, n = 19): a curious state improves memory, including for incidental material. **A** for associations; **B/C** for any effect on goal persistence.

## Implications for Adler

**Questions to add (once, at the phase named).**
- Goal formation: "Say the goal as a state you want, not a task." If the sentence is a means ("apply to jobs"), reflect the end back. Then: "Is this the kind of goal where the payoff only comes at the end, or where every session pays a bit?" If all-or-nothing, track the accumulative unit (sessions, pages) and shorten the horizon to weeks.
- Action design: "What would make the next ten minutes of this less bad, or slightly good?" Accept "nothing"; then offer bundling (CF rank 10) or company.
- Weekly review, growth goals only: "What was the most uncomfortable moment this week, and what did it teach you?"
- Weekly review, when adherence is high but the user reports dread: "If nobody would ever know, would you still do it?"
- Intake, optional: single items for procrastination ("I put off things I intend to do") and energy. Steel's data say energy, distractibility and automaticity explain most of the variance; Adler already collects the energy barrier and SRBAI (CF §6).

**Rules and thresholds.**
1. *Progress display is stateful.* Weeks 1–2 of an action, any week after a lapse, or confidence < 7: show what is done ("3 done"). Once automaticity ≥ 4/7 and adherence ≥ 80% over 14 days: show what remains ("2 to go"), since to-date framing licenses committed users. Never give both equal weight.
2. *Weekly reset plus a cumulative line for strong weeks.* The weekly count (D2, D3) is a Dai reset: good after a bad week, erasing after a good one. Prior week ≥ 80%: also show the running total ("41 of 52 since you started"). Prior week < 50%: show only the fresh week.
3. *Middle detection for project goals (D7).* At 40–60% of a dated project, offer to split the remainder into two dated steps; in the last 20%, hide subgoals and show the whole goal. Habits have no middle; do not import the concept.
4. *No fake head-starts.* Endowed progress is manipulation in a coach (EU AI Act Art. 5, CF §5). The legitimate form counts real prior evidence: "You've done this twice this month; that's the first two."
5. *Immediate-reward audit on "didn't feel like it".* Before WOOP (CF §2), ask what the next session would need to contain to be tolerable. Three consecutive "didn't feel like it" with no identifiable immediate reward joins the 3-in-14 revisit rule.
6. *Have-to profile.* If GDF B2 rulers score have-to > want-to, expect Milyavskaya's pattern: many obstacles, much reported effort, little progress. Log as a hypothesis; if three weeks of taps are dominated by "didn't feel like it" and "something came up" while the user says they are trying hard, say so: "You're working hard and it keeps getting blocked. Goals we hold because we should behave like that. Do you want it, or want to have done it?"
7. *Project deadlines: external, evenly spaced, coach-proposed, user-edited* (Ariely & Wertenbroch).
8. *Commitment devices stay opt-in and secondary* (CF rank 12): soft before hard; deposits only when the user asks for teeth; expect ~1 in 7 to accept and do not read refusal as low commitment.
9. *Bright lines as the user's own rules.* Ainslie's precedent mechanism means "never miss twice" and "the two-minute version always counts" work when stated back in the user's words, not imposed.
10. *Never prescribe grit, discipline or willpower.* Translate self-labels: "Most of that is design: where it sits in your day, how tempting the alternative is, how tired you are. Change one."
11. *Curiosity for learning goals only.* "What's one thing about this you don't know yet and would like to?" Do not gamify; the mystery/game effects are on curiosity scores, not persistence.
12. *Copy.* Safe to cite: Cerasoli 2014, Howard 2021, Steel 2007, Credé 2017, Lam & Zhou 2022, Halpern 2015. Never Fishbach or Woolley effect sizes.

**Adapting to one person over time.** Adler logs barrier → move → next seven days (CF §6). This file adds four person-level parameters estimable from that log, no new instrument needed: (a) *gradient sensitivity*: does adherence rise before a visible end (review, milestone) and drop after? If yes, keep horizons short and ends visible; if not, do not manufacture them. (b) *Frame response*: after switching to-date → to-go, did 14-day adherence rise or fall? Keep the winner per user. (c) *Reward locus*: does adherence track "enjoyed it" taps more than "important"? Tune for pleasantness or keep outcomes visible weekly accordingly. (d) *Effort profile*: high reported effort, low adherence, many obstacles is the have-to signature; low effort with steady adherence is the want-to signature and the cue to grow the action (AH Part C).

## Conflicts and open questions

- **Immediate rewards (Woolley & Fishbach 2018) vs undermining (Deci 1999).** Both experimental; they differ in the reward: an immediate bonus that makes the task feel better vs a salient contingent payment for a liked task. Evidence favours a boundary, not a winner. Adler uses only experiential, immediate, self-chosen rewards.
- **Goal gradient (monotonic) vs middle problem (U).** Bonezzi's model contains both: the shape depends on the reference point. Loyalty cards fix it at the end; life goals do not. Assume the U for projects, nothing for habits.
- **To-date framing licenses (Fishbach & Dhar) or commits (Koo & Fishbach 2008).** Resolved by commitment level, hence rule 1 keys on automaticity and adherence, not calendar time. Untested in a coaching app.
- **Present bias is small in money and over-reported (Imai 2021).** Effort studies (Augenblick) rescue it. Honest claim: people put off effort, not money.
- **Commitment: potent for accepters, low uptake, and Halpern's intention-to-treat result favours rewards.** Adler cannot pay rewards and should not push deposits; its structural lever (fixed slot, spaced deadlines) has the thinnest direct evidence of the three.
- **Grit is teachable (Alan 2019) yet barely predictive (Credé 2017).** Not a contradiction: the curriculum taught persistence after failure to children; it did not test trait grit.
- **Curiosity interventions raise curiosity (g = 0.57); none shows they raise goal persistence.** Open.
- **Energy (Gröpel & Steel; Steel 2018) overlaps Adler's "no energy" barrier and WHO-5 route.** Whether interest raises energy or tired people report both is unresolved; protect energy (sleep, timing) before treating it as boredom.
- **Nearly every Fishbach-lab result is single-lab, small-N and unreplicated by outsiders.** Direction plausible; magnitudes not quotable. The person-level tests above are how Adler finds out for its own users.
- **"Dry vs wet goals"** is not a Fishbach term in any retrieved source. Drop it or define it as ours.
