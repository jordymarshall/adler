# Depth and psychodynamic psychology for goal pursuit: what Adler can borrow

Research date: 2026-09-01. Scope: Freud, Jung, Alfred Adler, Horney, Winnicott/Kohut, attachment, schema therapy, TA drivers, IFS, Erikson/Marcia/Arnett, and the cognitive-science version of "unconscious motives", each assessed for (a) core idea from the primary text, (b) evidence status, (c) what a coach can borrow without practising therapy. Extends `docs/research/coaching-framework.md` §3-5 (MI, Adlerian encouragement vs praise, ACT/CBT borrowings, scope and crisis rules), which are not repeated.

Evidence labels: **Supported** (RCTs or large replicated correlational work), **Plausible** (consistent with adjacent evidence, no direct test), **Unfalsifiable** (no observation could count against it as stated), **Null** (tested and not found).

---

## 1. Freud and the psychodynamic tradition

**Core ideas.** Pleasure vs reality principle (1911): the mind first seeks immediate discharge; maturity is deferring for a surer later payoff. Defence mechanisms (Anna Freud 1936; Vaillant 1977): unconscious operations that keep threatening affects out of awareness. Repetition compulsion (1920): re-enacting rather than remembering. Superego (1923): internalised authority producing guilt disproportionate to any act. Secondary gain (1917; 1926): a symptom persists partly because of what it confers. Resistance (1914): whatever opposes the work, understood as protective, not perverse.

**Evidence.** Westen 1998 (*Psych Bulletin*) identified five propositions that mainstream research now supports: unconscious cognition, affect and motivation; ambivalence and parallel motives producing compromise solutions; childhood origins of dispositions; mental representations of self and others guiding relationships; developmental dynamics. Libido theory, psychosexual stages, the Oedipal account of neurosis and dream symbolism did not survive. Defences: Vaillant, Bond & Vaillant 1986 (*Arch Gen Psychiatry*; 307 men followed 40 years) found maturity of defences rated at 47 (sublimation, altruism, humour, anticipation, suppression vs projection, passive aggression, acting out, dissociation) "correlated highly" with independently assessed adult health and adjustment, more strongly the bleaker the childhood. Caveats: all-male, mostly Harvard samples; interview-rated; correlational and partly redundant with conscientiousness and low neuroticism. Repetition compulsion as a drive is Unfalsifiable; the phenomenon is better explained by attachment working models. Therapy efficacy: Shedler 2010 (*Am Psychologist*) reported a Cochrane meta-analysis (23 RCTs, N = 1,431) with pre-post d = 0.97, 1.51 at 9+ month follow-up (pre-post inflates; the long-term-therapy meta-analysis he also cited was shown by Bhar et al. 2010 to rest on miscalculated effect sizes). Steinert et al. 2017 (*AJP*; 23 RCTs, N = 2,751) formally tested equivalence to CBT/pharmacotherapy with a pre-set margin of g = 0.25 and found it (g = -0.15, 90% CI -0.23 to -0.08). Leichsenring et al. 2023 (*World Psychiatry*) umbrella review: psychodynamic therapy meets updated empirically-supported criteria for depressive, anxiety, personality and somatic disorders. Conclusion: the *therapy* works; that does not validate any specific Freudian mechanism, since common factors carry much of all psychotherapy outcome.

**Coach borrowings.** Pleasure/reality is present bias; say it behaviourally ("tonight-you and 7am-you want different things"). Secondary gain is the most useful lens: every stuck pattern is doing a job ("If this goal quietly disappeared, what would get easier?"). Resistance is the cue to stop pushing. Notice self-punishment after a miss and decline to join it. Never name defences, interpret childhood, or offer "unconscious" as an explanation.

## 2. Jung

**Core ideas.** Persona (social mask); shadow ("the thing a person has no wish to be", CW16); complexes (emotionally charged association clusters; Jung's 1904-09 word-association experiments were the empirical origin); individuation (lifelong integration toward wholeness); typology (*Psychological Types*, 1921); active imagination; archetypes and collective unconscious.

**Evidence.** Complexes prefigure implicit-association research; introversion/extraversion survives as Big Five Extraversion; archetypes, collective unconscious and individuation-as-telos are Unfalsifiable. MBTI fails psychometric standards: Pittenger 1993/2005 reports 39-76% of people receive a different type on retest at five weeks; dimensions are continuous and unimodal, not bimodal types; McCrae & Costa 1989 (N = 468) found the four scales track four Big Five traits (E/I-Extraversion, S/N-Openness, T/F-Agreeableness, J/P-Conscientiousness) while omitting Neuroticism, the trait most relevant to goal derailment, and concluded Jung's theory is "either incorrect or inadequately operationalized by the MBTI". Big Five is the evidence-based alternative; Conscientiousness is the best trait predictor of health behaviour and goal attainment (Bogg & Roberts 2004 meta-analysis). If Adler ever profiles users, a short Big Five instrument (BFI-2-S) is defensible; type labels are not.

**Coach borrowings.** The shadow is a metaphor for the "should" goal's disowned opposite: the user who wants to "stop being lazy" has a part that wants rest and has never been allowed to say so. "What does the part of you that skips know that the planning part doesn't?" is MI's double-sided reflection with a Jungian accent. Persona maps to goals adopted for how they look. Active imagination is not for a coach; its tame cousin is the "as if" question. Never type the user or mention archetypes.

## 3. Alfred Adler: individual psychology

**Core ideas** (Ansbacher & Ansbacher 1956; *Understanding Human Nature* 1927; *What Life Should Mean to You* 1931).
- *Teleology and fictional finalism.* Behaviour is pulled by goals, not pushed by drives: "We cannot think, feel, will, or act without the perception of some goal." Each person constructs a guiding fiction (Vaihinger's "as if"), an imagined state of security or significance that organises striving, mostly without awareness.
- *Inferiority feelings, striving for significance.* Universal childhood smallness drives compensation, healthily toward usefulness, unhealthily toward superiority over others or safeguarding through symptoms.
- *Style of life* and *private logic* (Dreikurs' term): unspoken convictions ("I am..., others are..., the world is..., therefore..."), set early and expressed in every movement. Mosak's *basic mistakes*: overgeneralisation ("nobody can be trusted"), impossible goals of security ("I must please everyone"), misperceptions of life's demands ("life is too hard"), denial of one's worth, faulty values.
- *Three life tasks*: work, love, community (Dreikurs and Mosak added self and spiritual).
- *Gemeinschaftsgefühl* (social interest): the criterion of health, the capacity to see with another's eyes and contribute; Adler's test of a goal is whether it lies on "the useful side of life".
- *Encouragement* over praise (coaching-framework §3); discouragement is the root of most failure.
- *The courage to be imperfect* (coined by Sofie Lazarsfeld at the 1925 congress, popularised by Dreikurs): perfectionism is a safeguarding tactic.
- *Early recollections* as projective data about the present style of life; *family constellation* and birth order as its shapers.
- *The Question*: "What would you do if you were well?" Adler used it to separate somatic from psychogenic complaints and to reveal what the symptom exempts the person from. Solution-focused therapists relabelled it the miracle question.
- Safeguarding: "yes, but", the hesitating attitude, distance-keeping: Adler's phenomenology of self-sabotage as goal-protection.

**Evidence.** Teleology is a stance, but its modern forms are Supported: goal-setting theory, self-concordance, and SDT's finding that intrinsic/communal aspirations predict wellbeing while extrinsic ones do not (Adler's useful side of life). Encouragement aligns with Deci 1999. Birth order: Null. Rohrer, Egloff & Schmukle 2015 (*PNAS*, N > 20,000, three countries): no effects on Big Five traits, a small first-born IQ advantage; Damian & Roberts 2015 (N = 377,000): mean absolute association with personality .02. Early recollections: reliable coding systems, no predictive-validity programme; Plausible at best. Adlerian *therapy* has case series and small trials; Adlerian *coaching* is described in the *Journal of Individual Psychology*, not tested. Influence is well documented: Ellis 1970, "Alfred Adler, more than Freud, is probably the true father of modern psychotherapy"; Beck credited Adler's cognitive emphasis; SFBT's miracle question and Watts's "reflecting as if" are Adlerian; positive psychology restates social interest.

**Verbatim Adlerian questions for the coach.**
- The Question: "Suppose this problem were gone tomorrow. What would you be doing that you aren't doing now?" Only if the user has already hinted: "So what does the problem let you skip?"
- Life tasks: "Across work, the people you love, and your wider world, where is the pinch right now?"
- Purpose (two levels, then stop): "When you have this, what will it get you? And what will that get you?"
- Useful side: "Who else is better off if you do this?"
- Encouragement: "You did it on a day you didn't want to. What got you there?"
- Courage to be imperfect: "What's the version you'd be slightly embarrassed by but would actually do?"
- Private logic, tentatively (Dreikurs' "Could it be...?", offered once): "Could it be the rule is 'properly or not at all'?"

## 4. Karen Horney

**Core ideas** (*Our Inner Conflicts* 1945; *Neurosis and Human Growth* 1950). Basic anxiety yields three moves: toward (compliance), against (mastery), away (detachment). The neurotic solution is an *idealised self* and the *search for glory* to actualise it. The **tyranny of the should** is the resulting inner dictatorship: "Forget about the disgraceful creature you actually are; this is how you should be; and to be this idealized self is all that matters." Shoulds ignore feasibility, admit no gradation, demand perfection now, and produce *neurotic pride* when met and *self-hate* when not. The *real self* is the "alive, unique, personal center" they silence.

**Evidence.** Supported by proxy. Shoulds became Ellis's "musturbation" and Beck's "should statements", both linked to depression and anxiety. Perfectionism research operationalises the idealised self: socially prescribed perfectionism (Hewitt & Flett) is the pathogenic dimension and rose about a third across 1989-2016 cohorts (Curran & Hill 2019, N = 41,641); Sirois et al. 2017 meta-analysis: perfectionistic concerns correlate r = .23 with procrastination, perfectionistic strivings r = -.22. Higgins 1987 self-discrepancy theory: actual-ought gaps predict agitation and guilt, actual-ideal gaps predict dejection. The three-moves typology has no psychometric base.

**Mapping to optimizer "should" goals.** Signals: should/need to/finally phrasing; no stated pleasure; disproportionate self-contempt after a miss; goals identical to a peer group's; goals "always" held, never started. Questions: "If nobody ever knew whether you did this, would you still want it?" "Whose voice says you should?" "What would 'enough' look like on an ordinary Tuesday?" Never say "neurotic".

## 5. Winnicott and Kohut

**Winnicott** (1960, "Ego Distortion in Terms of True and False Self"): the true self is the source of spontaneous gesture and feeling real; the false self is compliance built when the "good enough" caregiver could not meet the gesture. Pathology is when the false self is taken for the whole person and achievement feels hollow. **Kohut** (1971, 1977): the self is built from *selfobject* experiences of mirroring, idealising and twinship, internalised through *optimal frustration*; where these were absent the adult depends on external achievement for cohesion, and *narcissistic injury* (a slight, a failure) produces disproportionate shame and rage.

**Evidence.** Unfalsifiable as developmental accounts, but with Supported cousins: contingent self-worth (Crocker & Wolfe 2001, *Psych Review*: staking worth on a domain makes it a motivator and a vulnerability; Crocker & Park 2004: it raises stress and undermines learning), fragile vs secure high self-esteem (Kernis 2003), and SDT's introjected regulation, which predicts poorer persistence than identified/intrinsic regulation.

**Relevance.** "I am my output" is contingent self-worth; the daily unit (action, not outcome) is the structural corrective. Lenses: "How do you feel about yourself on a day you did nothing?"; separate "I did badly" from "I am bad"; "who is this goal for?" Never "false self", "narcissistic", or anything about parents.

## 6. Attachment theory

**Core ideas.** Bowlby (1969-80): the attachment system seeks a stronger, wiser other under threat; when satisfied it frees exploration; internal working models of self and other form from care received. Ainsworth 1978: secure, avoidant, ambivalent (Main added disorganised). Adults: Hazan & Shaver 1987 (romantic love as attachment); Bartholomew & Horowitz 1991; the ECR-R dimensions of anxiety and avoidance; Mikulincer & Shaver 2007/2016 model security as a resource for exploration and affect regulation.

**Evidence quality.** Supported with limits. Groh et al. 2017 meta-analytic review: early attachment predicts peer competence r = .19, externalising r = -.15, internalising r = -.07. Fraley 2002: infancy-to-adulthood stability is modest and fits a prototype model. Adult dimensions are reliable self-reports, only weakly related to infant measures. **Goal pursuit**: Elliot & Reis 2003 (*JPSP*, four studies): avoidance negatively predicts mastery-approach goals; anxiety predicts performance-avoidance and mastery-avoidance goals and fear of failure. Feeney 2004 and Feeney & Thrush 2010 (*JPSP*): a partner's availability, non-interference and encouragement (the three secure-base characteristics) strongly predict exploration and goal striving; intrusive help reduces it. **Alliance**: Diener & Monroe 2011 (17 samples, N = 886): security-alliance r = .17; Bernecker, Levy & Ellison 2014 (24 studies): avoidance r = -.14, anxiety r = -.12.

**Secure base as coach stance.** Feeney's criteria translate directly: reliably available (the check-in exists whether or not the action was done), non-intrusive (no fixing), encouraging (actions as experiments), distress before task. The coach never names attachment style; no coaching trial of style-matching exists. It can notice two derailers: the user who needs reassurance before every action (give structure, not reassurance) and the user who vanishes at the first miss (make re-entry one tap and shame-free).

## 7. Schema therapy (Young)

**Core ideas** (Young 1990; Young, Klosko & Weishaar 2003). Eighteen *early maladaptive schemas* formed when core childhood needs went unmet; coping by surrender, avoidance or overcompensation; *modes* as states (vulnerable child, punitive parent, demanding parent, detached protector, healthy adult). Goal-saboteur schemas: *unrelenting standards* ("nothing is good enough"), *failure*, *self-sacrifice*, *approval-seeking*, *insufficient self-control*, *negativity/pessimism*. The demanding-parent mode is the tyranny of the should as a state.

**Evidence.** Supported as therapy for personality disorders: Giesen-Bloo et al. 2006 (BPD, vs transference-focused, 3 years); Bamelis et al. 2014 (*AJP*; 12 Dutch sites, N = 323, cluster C/paranoid/histrionic/narcissistic PD; 50 sessions): blinded recovery at 3 years roughly 80% vs 60% (clarification-oriented) vs 50% (TAU), lower drop-out, less depression, better functioning; differences appeared on interview measures, not self-report. YSQ psychometrics are acceptable (Oei & Baranoff 2007). The unmet-needs etiology is Plausible, not tested.

**Noticing without labelling.** Track barrier tags and self-talk over weeks, not a questionnaire: "wasn't the proper version so didn't log it" (unrelenting standards); "knew I'd fail" (failure); own goal dropped whenever someone else needs something (self-sacrifice). Reflect as behaviour: "Three weeks running, what stopped you was that the plan wasn't the full version. Want to make the small version the official one?" Never "schema", "mode", "inner critic from childhood"; entrenched distressing patterns go to referral.

## 8. Transactional Analysis drivers (Kahler)

**Core ideas.** Kahler & Capers 1974 and Kahler 1975 (*TAJ*): five counter-injunctions observable in 1-7 second behaviours (*Be Perfect, Hurry Up, Try Hard, Please Others, Be Strong*), each with an "allower" (you are good enough; take your time; do it; please yourself; be open about wants).

**Evidence.** Weak. Vos & van Rijn 2022 pooled 41 TA trials with moderate effects (g ≈ .3-.9), mostly pre-post designs with few active-comparator RCTs; drivers themselves have questionnaires with modest reliability and no predictive-validity programme; the seconds-level detectability claim is untested. Treat as Plausible folk taxonomy overlapping schema therapy (Be Perfect = unrelenting standards; Please Others = self-sacrifice/approval; Be Strong = emotional inhibition; Try Hard = effort-not-completion; Hurry Up = urgency).

**Plain-language use.** Memorable and non-clinical: a fair internal lens and a usable user-facing metaphor stripped of jargon ("the rule seems to be 'I'll try', which never quite becomes 'Tuesday at 7'"). Never present as a validated typology.

## 9. Internal Family Systems (Schwartz)

**Core ideas** (Schwartz 1995; Schwartz & Sweezy 2020). The mind is multiple: *exiles* carry pain, *managers* protect proactively (perfectionist, planner, critic), *firefighters* react (bingeing, scrolling). Behind them the *Self* (calm, curiosity, compassion, confidence, courage, clarity, connectedness, creativity). Therapy unblends, befriends protectors, heals exiles.

**Evidence.** Small. Shadick et al. 2013 (*J Rheumatology*; N = 79 RA patients, 9-month IFS group vs education): better self-assessed pain, depressive symptoms and self-compassion at one-year follow-up, no effect on physician-rated disease activity, "proof-of-concept". Haddock et al. 2017 (N = 37 depressed college women, IFS vs CBT/IPT): both improved, no difference. Hodgdon et al. 2022: uncontrolled pilot, N = 17 with PTSD. A 2025 scoping review and a Society for Psychotherapy critique call the base "strikingly small" relative to adoption (45,000+ therapists listing it) and flag risks: application to reality-testing difficulties ("encouraging splitting of the self into parts... might be disorganizing"), false-memory litigation, and parts dialogue with complex trauma. The Self with eight Cs is Unfalsifiable.

**Parts language for goal conflict.** In its minimal form ("part of you wants X, part wants Y") it externalises ambivalence, reduces self-blame and is identical to MI's double-sided reflection and Westen's supported "compromise formation". Over-adoption risks: reification, trauma exposure (asking what a protector protects invites material the coach cannot hold), pseudo-clinical authority. Rule: two parts, one sentence, never origins, dropped if the response is distress rather than relief.

## 10. Erikson, Marcia, Arnett: identity for 16-40

**Core ideas.** Erikson (1950, 1968): identity vs role confusion (adolescence), intimacy vs isolation (young adulthood), generativity vs stagnation (from the late 30s). Marcia 1966: exploration and commitment yield diffusion, foreclosure (commitment without exploration), moratorium (exploration without commitment), achievement. Arnett 2000 (*Am Psychologist*): emerging adulthood (18-25, later 18-29) as identity exploration, instability, self-focus, feeling in-between, possibilities.

**Evidence.** The stage sequence is Unfalsifiable as epigenetic law; the constructs are measurable. Kroger, Martinussen & Marcia 2010 (124 studies): achievement rises through late adolescence and young adulthood, moratorium peaks at 19; in longitudinal samples 36% made progressive changes, 15% regressed, 49% stayed put. Generativity is measurable (Loyola Generativity Scale). Emerging adulthood describes educated, affluent cohorts; Côté 2014 argues it is a class artefact. The strongest developmental fact for this audience is not Eriksonian: Roberts, Walton & Viechtbauer 2006 (92 longitudinal samples) show Conscientiousness, Emotional Stability and Social Dominance rise most between 20 and 40, accelerated by committed roles. The optimizer is in the window where the habits being coached are also the traits being formed.

**Coach use.** Foreclosure produces inherited "should" goals; moratorium produces many goals and none started. Once: "Is this a goal you chose or inherited?" and "If you tried it for a month as an experiment, not a decision, what would you learn?" Never assign a stage or status.

## 11. Cross-cutting

**Resistance and ambivalence.** Freud located resistance in the patient; Adler saw safeguarding; Horney saw shoulds and their evasion; IFS sees protectors; schema therapy sees coping modes. All agree opposition to change is protective. MI dissolved "resistance" into *sustain talk* (normal ambivalence) and *discord* (a rupture usually produced by the helper). Magill 2018 (MI-inconsistent behaviour raises sustain talk, which predicts worse outcomes) vindicates every depth school's advice to stop pushing without their metapsychology. Adler's "yes, but" becomes a detection rule: agreement followed by a barrier means the coach has been arguing for change.

**Self-sabotage and fear of success.** *Self-handicapping* (Berglas & Jones 1978) is the Supported version: Schwinger et al. 2014 (49 effects, N = 25,550) find r = -.23 with achievement, driven by threatened self-esteem. *Fear of success* (Horner 1972) is Null-to-unmeasurable: its TAT-derived measures are unreliable and inter-correlate weakly (Tresemer 1977). What survives is fear of the *costs* of success (expectation, visibility, loss of the excuse): secondary gain by another name.

**The unconscious in cognitive science.** McClelland, Koestner & Weinberger 1989: implicit motives (from picture stories) and self-attributed motives (questionnaires) "seldom correlate" and predict different things, spontaneous long-run behaviour vs deliberate choices. Brunstein, Schultheiss & Grässmann 1998: progress on motive-congruent goals predicts wellbeing; commitment to incongruent goals predicts decline. Bargh's auto-motive model and Custers & Aarts 2010 (*Science*) claimed goals can be primed and pursued outside awareness; flagship social-priming results then failed direct replication (Doyen et al. 2012; Shanks et al. 2013), and current consensus treats goal priming as small and fragile while cue-triggered habit activation stands. Defensible reading for Adler: people are poor reporters of why they act; weeks of barrier tags reveal the operative goal better than the intake conversation. "Unconscious motive" is never offered to a user.

**Secondary gain reframed behaviourally.** Skipping is negatively reinforced (relief from anxiety, fatigue, looking bad) and often positively reinforced (the alternative activity). Kegan & Lahey's *Immunity to Change* (2009) formalises this for coaching as a *competing commitment* held by a *big assumption*; the evidence is case-based, but the four-column map is a coach-legitimate elicitation. Questions: "What does skipping give you that doing it doesn't?" "What would you have to risk if you did this every day?"

## The line between coaching and therapy

ICF: therapy "deals with healing pain, dysfunction and conflict"; coaching "supports personal and professional growth based on self-initiated change in pursuit of specific actionable outcomes" and "is future-focused". Its referral guidance names four triggers (impairment, unresolved history blocking progress, issues outside competence, safety); Code of Ethics standard 3.7 requires pausing and naming when therapy is the next step. The EMCC/AC Global Code of Ethics requires working within competence, recognising when a client needs a different service, and transparency about approach. Neither permits diagnosis, treatment or clinical formulation.

Rules for depth material in Adler:
1. **Borrow questions, not formulations.** "This comes from..." is formulating.
2. **Stay present- and future-facing.** Adler's own moves (the Question, "as if") are future-facing, which is why his material is safer than Freud's. Childhood, parents, early memories, birth order are out.
3. **One tentative reflection, then accept the answer.** Dreikurs' "could it be" is the model; certainty is interpretation.
4. **Patterns are about behaviour, not traits.** "Three weeks running..." not "you have a failure schema".
5. **Parts and shoulds language stops at relief.** Distress ends the frame; persistent distress triggers the referral script.
6. **Vocabulary blacklist** (extends coaching-framework §5): unconscious, defence/denial/projection, shadow, archetype, type labels, neurotic, idealised/false self, narcissistic, attachment style names, schema, mode, exile/manager/firefighter, driver names as labels, inner child, trauma, complex.
7. **Assume clinical distress is common.** Green, Oades & Grant 2006 found 52% and Spence & Grant 26% of people presenting for life-coaching studies in the clinical range on distress measures. When barrier tags cluster on mood, sleep loss, hopelessness or self-contempt, the hand-off protocol, not a depth lens, applies.

## Implications for Adler

- **Goal discovery.** Add a "should audit": should/need to/finally goals, or goals with no stated pleasure, get Horney's question and Adler's two-level purpose question before acceptance. Three-life-tasks pinch question as the domain scan.
- **One tiny action.** The courage-to-be-imperfect question is the standard way to shrink an action sized for the idealised self.
- **Check-in and barrier tags.** Add "wasn't the proper version" and "someone else needed me" as barrier categories; both are invisible in a tired/no-time/forgot taxonomy.
- **Weekly review.** Pattern reflection lives here, not in the daily check-in: one behavioural pattern, tentatively, one adjustment. Secondary-gain question as fallback after three weeks of the same barrier and failed practical fixes.
- **Re-engagement.** Design for the avoidant pattern: one tap, no shame, no mention of gap length.
- **Coach persona and safety.** Secure-base stance as the style guide; extend the blacklist; repeated self-contempt after misses is a referral signal, not material to explore.

## Conflicts and open questions

- **Efficacy vs mechanism.** Trials validate treatments, not constructs; common factors carry much of the effect. Borrow the stance (non-judgement, curiosity about function) more confidently than any construct.
- **Tentative reflection vs interpretation** is a matter of degree; it needs eval cases with graded examples, not a rule.
- **Users arriving with IFS vocabulary.** Meet them in their language or translate to plain ambivalence? Untested; default to translation.
- **Attachment-flavoured tailoring** of re-engagement copy is an A/B hypothesis, not a feature; no coaching trial exists.
- **Namesake risk.** Adler's signature constructs (style of life, inferiority, birth order) are untested or null. The defensible claim is that his *method* (teleological questions, encouragement, social interest, the Question, "as if") anticipated SFBT, CBT and SDT, where the evidence lives.
- **Revealed vs stated motives.** The design bet that barrier tags beat intake reasons is consistent with implicit-motive research but has no coaching test; instrument it.
