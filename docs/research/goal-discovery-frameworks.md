# Adler goal discovery: life-design frameworks, values methods, and a 10-minute intake flow

Research date: 2026-09-01. Scope: how a user with too many goals decides what to work on. Execution (implementation intentions, COM-B, MI, habits, WOOP) is in `docs/research/coaching-framework.md` and is not repeated; Part B plugs into that document's Phase 1 (Intake) and Phase 2 (Goal formation) and the schema in `docs/research/agent-engineering.md`. "No evidence" means no peer-reviewed outcome study of the framework itself was found, not that the idea is wrong.

---

## 0. Who is arriving

D4 fixes the audience: 16–40 optimizers with too many goals. What the literature says about them:

- **Multiple goals are the problem.** Kung & Scholer 2020: pursuing several goals at once is "challenging and often unsuccessful." Gray, Ozer & Rosenthal 2017 (meta-analysis, 54 studies): goal conflict predicts lower wellbeing and, more strongly, distress.
- **Perfectionism is rising.** Curran & Hill 2019 (146 samples, N = 41,641): socially prescribed perfectionism rose about a third from 1989 to 2016.
- **Comparison is the channel.** Upward comparison on social media correlates r ≈ .33 with maladjustment (2026 meta-analysis, N = 36,583).
- **Output-contingent self-worth.** Crocker & Park 2004: staking self-esteem on a domain makes setbacks hit harder and drives self-validation and self-handicapping. "Productivity dysmorphia" (Codrea-Rado 2021, journalism) is the felt version.
- **Baseline strain.** Deloitte 2024: about 40% of Gen Z and millennials are stressed all or most of the time; pooled workaholism prevalence is 15.2% (Andersen et al. 2023).
- **ADHD.** CDC 2024: 6.0% of US adults, half diagnosed in adulthood. The share in productivity audiences is unmeasured; treat it as a design assumption, never a label.
- **Healthy Gamer profile.** No published demographics; qualitatively young, online, self-aware, shame-driven avoidance, "I know what to do but don't do it."

Implication: this user needs no framework that generates goals, only help learning which goals are theirs, which are borrowed, which fight, and which to start with, without feeling they have failed the rest.

## Part A. Frameworks

### 1. Ikigai

The four-circle Venn (love, good at, world needs, paid for) was posted by Marc Winn on 14 May 2014, merging Andrés Zuzunaga's 2012 "purpose" diagram with a word from Dan Buettner's Blue Zones. Winn: "I didn't invent the diagram. I didn't invent ikigai. I just put them in the same picture, on a small personal blog, on a Wednesday morning." The Japanese concept differs. Mieko Kamiya (1966) defined ikigai-kan as feeling life is moving forward, with seven needs including growth, a bright future, resonance (belonging) and freedom; Ken Mogi (2017) gives five pillars: start small, release yourself, harmony, the joy of little things, being here and now. Both say ikigai is ordinary, plural and unconnected to pay.

**Evidence:** Japanese cohort data link self-reported ikigai to lower mortality; the diagram has none. **Use:** Kamiya's "bright future" and "resonance" are a good check on a goal list; Mogi's "start small" matches the tiny-action protocol. **Drop:** the Venn, which implies one grand purpose at the centre of four constraints, the belief that paralyses optimizers. **Questions:** "Which of these, if it moved even slightly, would make you feel life is going somewhere?" "Which one involves people you want more of?"

### 2. Sahil Bloom, *The 5 Types of Wealth* (2025)

Time, Social, Mental, Physical, Financial wealth; "our scoreboard is broken." Tools: **Wealth Score** (25 statements, 0–4 each, max 100, "screenshot and return"); **energy calendar** (colour past events green/red/yellow by energy, raise the green:red ratio); **Life Razor** (one identity sentence to cut decisions; his: "I will coach my son's sports teams"); **anti-goals** (what each big goal must not cost).

**Evidence:** none; marketing cites "thousands of interviews." The score is unvalidated. **Use:** the energy calendar is a cheap Riediger & Freund interference item; anti-goals reframe goal conflict; the Life Razor is a self-concordance test in disguise. **Drop:** "wealth" framing is hustle-coded and puts Financial on a par with relationships, the reverse of the aspirations evidence (section 10); no score that invites optimising all five. **Questions:** "What last week left you with more energy than you started with? What drained you?" "What are you not willing to give up for this?"

### 3. Arthur Brooks

Happiness = enjoyment, satisfaction, meaning; the "happiness portfolio" is faith (transcendence, not necessarily religion), family, friends and earned, serving work; the "idols" are money, power, pleasure, fame. *From Strength to Strength* (2022): fluid intelligence peaks in the 30s, crystallised keeps rising, so strivers should jump curves; the "reverse bucket list" crosses off wants.

**Evidence:** survey synthesis, no intervention trial; the curves rest on cognitive-ageing data. **Use:** the idols list is Kasser & Ryan's extrinsic aspirations in plain English; the reverse bucket list is a legitimate subtraction exercise. **Drop:** "faith" as a label; the second-curve argument concerns people past 40. **Questions:** "If you got this and nobody ever knew, would you still want it?" "Which could you cross off, not because you'd fail, but because you don't want it any more?"

### 4. Wheel of Life

Paul J. Meyer, Success Motivation Institute, 1960s: rate 8 life areas 0–10, draw the wheel, raise the low spokes. **Evidence:** none (Swart 2022 is conceptual). **Use:** the cross-domain audit, which the VLQ and Bullseye do with validated instruments (section 10). **Drop:** balance scoring tells an optimizer to fix every low spoke, which manufactures goals. **Questions:** the Bullseye's: "Where are you living closest to what matters to you? Where furthest? Does the gap bother you, or is it fine for this season?"

### 5. Burnett & Evans, *Designing Your Life* (Stanford, 2016)

Design thinking applied to life: a 250-word **Workview** and **Lifeview** checked for clashes; a **Good Time Journal** (engagement and energy per activity); three **Odyssey Plans** (current path; the path if that vanished; the path if money and image didn't matter); **prototype** conversations and experiences before committing; "gravity problems" (unchangeable circumstances) are not problems.

**Evidence:** Stanford's Life Design Lab reports course outcomes (career self-efficacy and agency up, career anxiety and dysfunctional beliefs down); a 2025 review calls the base emerging; no RCT. "Life-design counselling" (Savickas) has controlled studies but is a different method with the same name. **Use:** the third Odyssey plan is the best "should" detector in this survey; prototyping is the right posture for an uncertain goal and is what the tiny action already is. **Drop:** the worksheets. **Questions:** "If money and what other people think weren't a factor, which of these would still be on the list?" "Which could you try for two weeks without deciding anything?"

### 6. James Clear, *Atomic Habits*

"Every action you take is a vote for the type of person you wish to become"; "you do not rise to the level of your goals, you fall to the level of your systems." **Evidence:** habit mechanics borrow Lally and Wood & Neal; the identity claim has no direct trial and Gollwitzer et al. 2009 found announcing identity goals reduces effort. **Use:** "systems over goals" is the rule that Adler stores an outcome in `goals.title` but coaches an action; "who would this make you?" surfaces borrowed identities fast. **Drop:** identity badges and declarations. **Questions:** "If you did this for a year, what would it say about you?" "Is that something you want to be, or think you're supposed to be?"

### 7. Oliver Burkeman, *Four Thousand Weeks* (2021), *Meditations for Mortals* (2024)

Time management as denial of finitude; life is not a problem to solve. Tools: a fixed-volume closed list (at most ten open commitments; nothing enters until something leaves); "strategic underachievement" (decide in advance what to be bad at); a done list; "dailyish" rules, not streaks; imperfectionism.

**Evidence:** none claimed, yet it is the only popular text consistent with Milyavskaya & Inzlicht (willpower does not predict attainment), Silverman & Barasch (streaks turn a miss into a quit) and Mayer & Freund (shelving beats abandoning). **Use:** the closed list is the interface for parked goals; "what will you be mediocre at this season?" is the best prioritisation question for this audience because it grants permission; "dailyish" is the language for "4 of 6 this week." **Warn:** the register can read bleak in an app. **Questions:** "Which of these are you choosing to be bad at for the next couple of months?" "If only one could move this season and the rest had to wait, which one?"

### 8. Anne-Laure Le Cunff, *Tiny Experiments* (2025)

Replace outcome goals with experiments. A **pact**: "I will [action] for [duration]" (10 days to 3 months), tracked yes/no, read as data. **PACT**: Purposeful, Actionable ("outputs you can control"), Continuous ("simple and repeatable"), Trackable ("have you done the thing or not"). Three "cognitive scripts" behind borrowed goals: Sequel (what follows from past choices), Crowd-pleaser (what others expect), Epic (what a grand narrative demands).

**Evidence:** none for the package; a pact is a time-boxed process goal (Locke & Latham), binary tracking is progress monitoring (Harkin 2016). **Use:** the three scripts are a ready taxonomy for "should" goals; the pact bridges into Phase 3. **Warn:** experimenting can become its own perfectionism; keep the review to "what did you learn?" **Questions:** "Is this here because it's the next chapter of something you started, because someone expects it, because it sounds impressive, or because you'd like doing it?" "What's the two-week version you could run just to find out?"

### 10. Values clarification with evidence

**ACT: Valued Living Questionnaire** (Wilson, Sandoz, Kitchens & Roberts 2010): ten domains (family, intimate relationships, parenting, friendship, work, education, recreation, spirituality, citizenship, physical self-care), each rated 1–10 for importance and for the past week's consistency with it; validated. **Bull's-Eye Values Survey** (Lundgren et al. 2012): four domains (work/education, leisure, relationships, personal growth/health), distance-from-centre plus an obstacle rating; validated. Rule: a goal in an important, inconsistent domain is a candidate; one in an unimportant domain is a "should" candidate.

**Schwartz values** (1992; 19-value refinement 2012, N = 6,059): values sit on a circle, adjacent compatible, opposite conflicting (achievement vs benevolence). Use only to explain why two goals fight; do not administer the PVQ.

**Self-concordance model** (Sheldon & Elliot 1999; replicated across cultures and in sport). Per goal, four reasons rated 1–9: external ("because somebody else wants you to, or because you'll get something from somebody if you do"), introjected ("because you would feel ashamed, guilty, or anxious if you didn't; you feel that you ought to"), identified ("because you really believe it is an important goal to have; you endorse it freely and value it whole-heartedly"), intrinsic ("because of the fun and enjoyment the goal provides"). Concordance = (identified + intrinsic) − (external + introjected). Concordant goals get sustained effort and more attainment, and attaining them raises need satisfaction and wellbeing; attaining non-concordant goals does not. **Koestner, Lekes, Powers & Chicoine 2002:** self-concordance and implementation intentions each predict progress and **interact**: if-then plans help most on goals that are the person's own. This is the key finding for Adler: the WOOP machinery in Phases 2–3 pays off mainly when Phase 1 picked a concordant goal.

**Personal Projects Analysis** (Little 1983): elicit current projects, rate each on meaning, manageability, connection and affect; wellbeing is "the sustainable pursuit of core projects." The ancestor of every list-then-rate tool.

**Best Possible Self** (Carrillo et al. 2019 meta-analysis, 29 studies, N = 2,909): wellbeing d = .33, optimism d = .33, positive affect d = .51. It works as reflection; fantasy about a specific goal without an obstacle does not (Kappes & Oettingen). One sentence, not a session.

**Future/Self-Authoring.** Morisano et al. 2010 (RCT, N = 85 struggling students, four months): imagine the ideal future, list 6–8 goals, rank, elaborate, plan obstacles; GPA rose significantly and the share falling below full-time enrolment went from 20% to 0%. Schippers, Scheepers & Peterson 2015 (N = 703) and Schippers et al. 2023 replicated at scale. The active steps (elaborate, rank, name obstacles, write) are discovery itself. Use the structure; do not cite Peterson's commentary.

### 11. Goal-systems research

- **Goal systems** (Kruglanski et al. 2002): goals and means form a network. **Multifinality**: one means serves several goals. Rule: pick the most multifinal action first.
- **Goal shielding** (Shah, Friedman & Kruglanski 2002, six studies): commitment to a focal goal inhibits alternatives, less so when they facilitate it. One committed goal quiets the others; six equally committed goals shield nothing.
- **Conflict** (Emmons & King 1988): conflict between strivings (rated −2 to +2 pairwise) predicted negative affect, depression, somatic complaints and clinic visits over a year, and rumination rather than action. Riediger & Freund 2004: interference (time, energy, money, incompatible strategies) and facilitation are separate; interference lowers wellbeing, facilitation raises engagement. Gray et al. 2017 confirms.
- **Shelving vs disengaging** (Mayer & Freund 2022, N = 214): shelving reduces conflict as much as abandoning, with less regret.
- **Approach vs avoidance** (Elliot & Sheldon 1997; Elliot, Sheldon & Church 1997): more avoidance goals ("stop procrastinating") predict declining wellbeing and worse performance over a semester.
- **Intrinsic vs extrinsic aspirations** (Kasser & Ryan 1996; Bradshaw et al. 2023 meta-analysis): growth, relationships, community and health predict wellbeing; wealth, fame and image predict lower wellbeing, across cultures.

### 12. Healthy Gamer / Dr K on purpose

HG wiki and coaching pack: dharma "means duty or responsibility"; "Dharma is a meeting between who you are and what the world asks of you." Intent has four sources: desire, pressure ("should" and culture), values, duty. Exercise: compare "things you want vs things you care about." On burnout: "burnout is not about working too much, it's about working ineffectively." On optimizers: the "performance trap" (conditional regard produces adults whose worth is contingent on achievement).

**Evidence:** interpretive. The four intents map onto the self-concordance continuum (desire ≈ intrinsic, should ≈ introjected, values ≈ identified, duty ≈ identified plus obligation); the performance trap is Crocker & Park. **Use:** the want-vs-care-about contrast is a one-question exercise; the burnout line is a non-blaming frame for a grinding user (direction, not effort). Do not use "dharma" in copy or repeat the claim that living by duty treats depression. **Questions:** "Of these, which do you want, and which do you actually care about? They're different lists."

## Part B. Synthesis for Adler

### B1. Principles the evidence supports

Self-concordance first, plans second (Koestner 2002). One committed goal shields the others (Shah 2002); parked goals stay visible, uncounted, shelved not deleted (Mayer & Freund). Conflict is one question per pair, and facilitation is a reason to choose (Riediger & Freund). Convert avoidance to approach before storing (Elliot). Extrinsic "why"s are a yellow flag, not a veto (Kasser & Ryan). Listing, ranking and writing is itself the intervention (Morisano; Schippers).

### B2. The 10-minute flow for a user with six goals

One question per turn, reflections between, no supplied reasons. Questions are verbatim-ready.

**Step 0. Dump (≈1 min).** "Tell me everything you've been meaning to work on. Don't rank them or justify them, just list." Each becomes a `goals` row, `status = 'candidate'`, user wording in `title`. If more than eight: "That's a lot to carry. We keep all of it; we're only choosing where to start."

**Step 1. Values pass (≈2 min).**
- "Looking at that list, which one would bother you most to be in the same place with a year from now?" (Little's meaning dimension; a one-sentence regret-framed BPS.)
- "What would that give you that you don't have right now?" (`goals.why`; if the answer is an outcome, once more: "and what would that do for you?" then stop.)
- Reflect: "So the thread is [value]." Store `memories.kind = 'identity'` ("values being someone friends can rely on"). A neglected domain ("I've let health go") goes to `memories.kind = 'life_context'`.

**Step 2. Should-detection (≈2 min).** Top two or three candidates only. Two 0–10 rulers adapted from the self-concordance items:
- "0 to 10, how much is [goal] there because you'd feel guilty, judged or behind if you dropped it?" (controlled)
- "0 to 10, how much because you genuinely value it or would enjoy doing it?" (autonomous)
- **Rule: concordance = autonomous − controlled; if ≤ 0, label `should` and do not start there.** If both are high (common here): "If nobody ever knew you'd done it, would you still want it?" A "no" moves it to `should`. Titles containing "should", "need to", "supposed to", "by now", "everyone", or a named comparison person are `should` until the rulers say otherwise.
- Script: "That one sounds like it belongs to someone else's list. We can park it, or you can tell me what you'd want instead." Store the answer as `memories.kind = 'preference'`.

**Step 3. Approach conversion (≈30 s, when triggered).** For stop/quit/less/avoid titles: "What would you be doing instead, in the time that frees up?" Approach phrasing goes to `goals.title`; the original wording to `goals.why`.

**Step 4. Conflict and facilitation (≈2 min).** For the remaining two or three:
- "If you did [A] most days this month, which of the others would get less of your time or energy?" (IRQ interference, time and energy only.)
- "Is there one that, if it went well, would make the others easier?" (multifinality.)
- **Rule: score = facilitation count − interference count; highest wins; ties go to higher concordance, then higher confidence.** A goal that interferes with two or more and facilitates none is not a starting point; say so and offer: "We could try a tiny version for two weeks and see what it costs the others."

**Step 5. Choose and park (≈1.5 min).**
- "It sounds like [A] is the one. Say it back in your own words." (Overwrites `goals.title`.)
- "0 to 10, how confident are you that you could start this week?" (existing ruler; < 7 shrinks in Phase 3.)
- "The other [n] aren't gone. They stay on your list as parked, with nothing counting against them, and I'll ask about them again on [next fresh-start landmark, four or more weeks out]. Anything you'd rather drop entirely?" Set `status = 'shelved'`; `dropped` only on the user's say-so (`goals.status` is free text; these are proposed values).
- If the user resists parking: "Which of these are you choosing to be bad at until then?"

**Step 6. Hand-off (≈1 min).** "Now the inside part." Run the existing Phase 2 obstacle question ("What inside you usually gets in the way? Not your schedule, you.") into `goals.obstacle_inner`. A self-label ("I'm lazy") is reflected as a thought and stored nowhere.

Total 9–10 minutes. One-goal arrivals run Steps 1, 2, 3, 6 (≈4 min); quick-add arrivals (D2) get Step 2 alone.

### B3. Output mapping

| Output | Field | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Each goal, user's wording | `goals.title` | Approach-framed; the chosen one overwritten by the user's own sentence |
| Value or need behind it | `goals.why` | User's words; may hold the original avoidance phrasing |
| Inner obstacle | `goals.obstacle_inner` | Step 6, unchanged from existing WOOP |
| Chosen / parked / dropped | `goals.status` | `active` (one), `shelved`, `dropped`, `candidate` |
| Concordance and conflict notes | `memories.kind = 'preference'` | "Goal X felt imposed (guilt 8, value 3)"; "X interferes with Y on time" |
| Values thread | `memories.kind = 'identity'` | Positive, user-endorsed only; never labels |
| "Don't coach me on X" | `memories.kind = 'preference'` | Honoured by `planner` and `nudge_writer` |
| Parked-goal return date | `interventions.type = 'revisit_goal'`, scheduled | Uses the fresh-start landmark rule |

Add to the monthly review: "Any of the parked ones asking for attention, or can they stay parked?" and re-run Step 2 on anything pulled back.

### B4. Life domains

Options: 5 Types of Wealth; Wheel of Life (8); Brooks (4); VLQ (10); Bullseye (4); custom. **Recommendation: four domains as tags, not scores, derived from the Bullseye and the intrinsic-aspiration set** (Wheel of Life's eight collapse into the same four).

| Adler domain | Contains | Bullseye | VLQ | 5 Types |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Health | sleep, movement, food, energy | growth/health | physical self-care | Physical |
| People | partner, family, friends, community | relationships | family, intimate, parenting, friendship, citizenship | Social |
| Work and learning | career, study, money as a means | work/education | work, education | Financial, part of Mental |
| Self | play, rest, creativity, meaning | leisure | recreation, spirituality | Time, part of Mental |

Rationale: four fits three screens; the set maps to two validated instruments so VLQ-style questions can be asked later without re-tagging; money sits inside Work so an extrinsic aspiration is not a peer of relationships (Kasser & Ryan); Self gives rest and play a home; "faith" and "wealth" vocabularies are avoided. No balance score, no wheel: an empty domain informs the coach's Step 1 question and never prompts a goal.

### B5. Don'ts for this audience

1. Do not generate goals: no suggestions, no domain-gap prompts, no "you haven't set a health goal."
2. Do not score life (no wheel, wealth score or balance meter) and do not show parked goals with counts, bars or "days since."
3. Do not gamify identity: no "you're becoming a runner," no identity declarations (Gollwitzer 2009).
4. Do not let `should` goals through silently; flag once, plainly, then let the user decide.
5. Do not reward the dump; six goals is not an achievement.
6. Do not use optimise, maximise, hack, grind, level up, unlock or potential in coach copy.
7. Do not answer "how can I do all six?" with a schedule. Run Step 4 and the Burkeman question; if the user insists, offer a two-week tiny version of one.
8. Do not compare to other users, and do not set reflection homework (no essays, odyssey plans or default journaling).
9. Do not treat a self-label as data, and do not read "no energy" plus a long list as a motivation problem: it is the goal-conflict signature; go to Step 4, then the WHO-5 route if it persists.
10. Do not cite any framework in user-facing copy. Users get questions, not brands.

### B7. Open questions

The Step 2 rulers compress a 4-item 1–9 instrument to two 0–10 items; validate against the full items in an early cohort before trusting the ≤ 0 rule. The interference question covers time and energy only. Whether parked-goal return dates are shown to the user or only to the planner is a UX call.
